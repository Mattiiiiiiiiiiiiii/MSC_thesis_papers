a b s t r a c t
The maturation of several enabling technologies has brought aircraft conceptualization back to the forefront of
modern engineering systems design. Unfortunately, the development of tools capable of assessing unconventional
conﬁgurations and powertrain architectures has not kept pace. To accelerate progress in the multidisciplinary
design and analysis of novel aircraft, we introduce the Research Community Aerospace Interdisciplinary Design
Environment (RCAIDE), a Python-based, open-source platform for modeling, analyzing, and optimizing both conventional and unconventional aerospace systems. The successor to the globally recognized SUAVE code, RCAIDE
builds on a robust foundation of methods while extending its scope to address novel design paradigms that constitute emerging technologies. RCAIDE’s modular structure supports high-performance parallel computing and
multi-ﬁdelity analysis, thereby unlocking computationally intensive operations such as uncertainty quantiﬁcation
and generative artiﬁcial intelligence applications. This paper documents the validation of the most frequently
employed methods across various disciplines within the tool and demonstrates how a novel, generalized approach
to deﬁning energy networks can be leveraged to model both conventional and emerging propulsion architectures.
A collection of case studies spanning diﬀerent aircraft classes is subsequently presented to highlight the code’s
versatility and value to academia, industry, government, and the broader aerospace community.

1. Introduction
Since the early 1960s, computational tools have played a pivotal
role in shaping aircraft design. By the 2000s, advances in computer
memory and processing power had enabled engineers to address increasingly complex problems [1–4]. With growing interest in alternative aviation fuels, electric hybridization, distributed propulsion unlocking urban air mobility, and supersonic ﬂight, the demand for tools
that can rapidly parametrize, analyze, and optimize vehicle conﬁgurations has never been greater. Yet, the applicability of many oncefoundational tools in the designer’s toolbox has been constrained by
limited parametric ﬂexibility and insuﬃcient adaptability to emerging
technologies.
At present, publicly available tools can be broadly categorized into
two groups: discipline-speciﬁc and aircraft-synthesis tools. Disciplinespeciﬁc tools focus on one or a few tightly coupled disciplines, namely
aerodynamics [5–8], acoustics [9,10], aerostructural [11,12], propulsion [13,14], and optimization [15,16]. Aircraft synthesis tools, by contrast, emphasize the interactions among subsystems. To achieve this,

they typically employ lower ﬁdelity models to capture vehicle-level behavior and performance [17–23].
Initially supported under a NASA LEARN Program, SUAVE [24] was
developed to address the shortcomings of sacriﬁcing ﬁdelity in studying
large engineering systems. Released as an open-source project in 2014, it
was widely adopted; however, it eventually exhibited the growing pains
typical of most design tools-code maintenance and extensibility. Because
it was maintained within a single academic institution, development
and maintenance were tied to the student workforce, which meant that
turnover hampered progress. Second, as global participation expanded,
it became increasingly apparent that a structure that better attributes
external contributions was needed. Third, with the monumental shift
from Python 2 to 3, advances in data structures and high-performance
computing could not be fully leveraged without a clean-sheet refactoring of the code’s core architecture. Finally, license restrictions emerged
as a major barrier for companies seeking to conduct proprietary work,
limiting industrial uptake.

suﬀer from ﬂaws in their fundamental powertrain logic that demand a
paradigm shift in methodology. As alternative energy systems become
more common in aircraft, secondary components like pumps, heat exchangers, and tanks-especially in cryogenic engines-are emerging as key
bottlenecks. These elements consume power and add losses that must be
considered in the overall system balance. Most aircraft design tools focus
on ﬂight dynamics and use open-loop methods for modeling powertrain
performance. For example, when simulating a conventional turbofan engine, a mission solver typically calculates fuel ﬂow based on the throttle
setting but doesn’t initially account for the extra power required by the
pumps. This pump power can only be calculated once the fuel ﬂow is
known, so designers often back-calculate the additional fuel required to
power the pump. However, this method doesn’t adjust engine eﬃciency
for the added load. As powertrains become more complex, especially
with cryogenic fuels and multiple energy conversions, these open-loop
methods can lead to design ﬂaws. Issues such as pressure changes, state
transitions, and thermal losses in cryogenic tanks further complicate
matters. To address these challenges, new approaches are needed for
modeling powertrains during early aircraft design. As a result, in July
2023, SUAVE’s development was sunset in favor of a successor – the
Research Community Aerospace Interdisciplinary Design Environment
(RCAIDE).
RCAIDE is an interdisciplinary computational research tool designed
to facilitate the conceptual design of novel aerospace systems by integrating modeling, simulation, analysis, and optimization into a cohesive framework. Although RCAIDE and SUAVE share a common lineage,
they exhibit fundamental diﬀerences. RCAIDE supplants SUAVE’s basic
NumPy/SciPy core with an optional automatic diﬀerentiation (AD) numerical backend used across solvers and optimizers, producing exact
derivatives, accelerating convergence, and enabling substantially larger
gradient-based optimization problems. This new backend leverages existing CUDA AD libraries developed for AI training to provide native
GPU execution and scalable performance. In terms of powertrain modeling, SUAVE explicitly deﬁnes energy networks, limiting ﬂexibility to
deﬁning custom source-propulsor couplings. In contrast, RCAIDE uses
an energy-agnostic network, enabling designers to build a wide range of
architectures, including those beyond conventional classiﬁcations. Unlike SUAVE, RCAIDE oﬀers improved aerodynamic drag estimation and
modernizes dated methods for aircraft noise prediction. Furthermore,
RCAIDE employs a medium-ﬁdelity stability model based on a vortex
lattice method (VLM) perturbation approach, which enables the computation of stability derivatives for unconventional aircraft. This represents an advancement over the semi-empirical methods typically used
for conventional tube-and-wing conﬁgurations. Regarding component
modeling, RCAIDE oﬀers higher-ﬁdelity models for key powertrain elements, such as cryogenic tanks, heat exchangers, and fuel cells, thereby
providing greater granularity for optimization. The mission solver has
been refactored to support multiple solvers, some of which may require
more computational time but oﬀer greater robustness and the ability to
address six-degrees-of-freedom (6DOF) problems, as well as underconstrained scenarios where the number of unknowns exceeds the number
of coupling equations. For example, this includes trimming a six-rotor
electric vertical takeoﬀ and landing (eVTOL) aircraft, where each rotor
may have unique rotational speeds, thrust vectoring angles, and blade
pitches. RCAIDE is built from the ground up to sustain long-term growth
and collaboration. Spanning universities and commercial entities worldwide, it is supported by version-controlled repositories, unit testing, and
continuous integration via GitHub [25], ensuring method validation and
veriﬁcation.

2. Code architecture and philosophy
Deliberate eﬀorts were undertaken to preserve the essential elements of the software architecture and coding philosophies that enabled RCAIDE’s predecessor to remain viable for nearly a decade. The
code is structured into hierarchical repositories that contain native data
structures, functions, component abstractions, and subroutines that implement methods associated with the various disciplines, e.g., atmosphere, geodesics, mass properties, aerodynamics, stability, aerostructures, powertrain, emissions, acoustics, and performance. As illustrated
in Fig. 1, the primary subdirectory, RCAIDE, housing the source code,
is organized into two tertiary subdirectories: Framework and Library.
Framework deﬁnes foundational data structures and core class hierarchies, whereas Library provides abstractions of physical components
and analytical methods. Aircraft conceptualization within RCAIDE begins with the instantiation of the Vehicle() Python class, which functions as a hierarchical container for mass and inertial properties, geometric speciﬁcations, component deﬁnitions, and aircraft classiﬁcation
(e.g., transport, commuter). The class autonomously identiﬁes components such as wings, fuselages, booms, nacelles, cargo bays, landing
gear, and powertrain architectures as they are programmatically added
and organizes them into predeﬁned taxonomic categories within the vehicle hierarchy.
Method-attribute orthogonality enables the coexistence and interchangeability of multiple modeling ﬁdelities without compromising the
integrity of the overall simulation architecture. For example, the wing
lift coeﬃcient can be estimated using either a ﬁrst-order approach that
combines thin-airfoil theory with ﬁnite-wing corrections for downwash,
or a higher-ﬁdelity computational ﬂuid dynamics solver based on the
Euler equations, both of which employ the same wing deﬁnition. Moreover, RCAIDE enables users to interchange methods across mission segments to achieve requisite accuracy while minimizing computational
cost. For instance, when evaluating a novel aircraft conﬁguration, a
medium-ﬁdelity VLM coupled with empirical drag buildup approaches
may provide adequate approximations of cruise performance characteristics. In low-speed ﬂight conditions with deﬂected control surfaces,
higher-ﬁdelity aerodynamic models may be required to accurately capture ﬂow separation.
The Verification & Validation subdirectory ensures that 1) during code development, methods are not inadvertently modiﬁed to
produce erroneous outputs, and 2) models generate approximations
consistent with experimental measurements. Finally, the Tutorials
subdirectory provides a comprehensive collection of example workﬂows
to facilitate onboarding for new users and demonstrate optimal software
utilization.

Vortex lattice method with semi-empirical drag buildup
This medium-ﬁdelity potential ﬂow solver, coupled with drag corrections, serves as the default approach to compute aerodynamic forces
on all vehicle surfaces, including wings, control surfaces, and lofted bodies such as fuselages, nacelles, and booms. Based on the original Fortran
implementation of VORLAX [54], this reformulated VLM can compute
inviscid aerodynamic forces and moments across all regimes-subsonic,
transonic, and supersonic-on all vehicle surfaces, including wings, control surfaces, and lofted bodies such as fuselages, nacelles, and booms.
It provides reasonably accurate predictions compared to higher-ﬁdelity
methods at a fraction of the computational cost. Features are modeled
along the axis of symmetry, or for wings, along their mean camber line,
then discretized into chordwise and spanwise panels. Each panel possesses a horseshoe vortex made up of a bound vortex at the 1/4 chord
location with trailing legs extending to inﬁnity behind the panel. The
control point on each panel where aerodynamic loads are computed
is located at the 3/4 chord location. The induced velocity on a control
point on the wing is determined by integrating the inﬂuence of the entire system of circulation strengths. The lift on each surface is found by
decomposing the normal forces acting on it relative to the ﬂow, while a
Treﬀtz-plane approach is used to determine the induced drag.

components, ranging from friction drag to pressure drag sources, drag
emerging from compressible and shock waves, and the drag associated
with the operating state of the aircraft.

2D viscous vortex panel method
The viscous vortex panel method is used to model and compute the
forces acting on airfoils. It combines an inviscid panel method based on
Hess and Smith [68] with a numerical solution of the boundary-layer
equations on the airfoil surface. Moran [69] provides detailed information on the creation of the inﬂuence coeﬃcient and ﬂow tangency matrices needed to solve the inviscid ﬂow around an airfoil. The boundary
layer equations solved here are the 2D Von Karman integral boundary
layer equations, along with suitable closure equations based on whether
the ﬂow is laminar or turbulent over that speciﬁc portion of the airfoil
surface. In the case of laminar ﬂow, the combined system of integral
boundary-layer equations and the closure equations used is Thwaites’
method [30] while Head’s method [70] is used within the turbulent
regime. The location of transition from laminar ﬂow to turbulent ﬂow
is computed using the Michel criterion [71] in RCAIDE. This wake modeling is achieved using the Squire-Young model [72].

Energy Sources, and Distributors. Converters represent physical components that transform energy between distinct domains.
Propulsors constitute assemblies of converters operating synergistically to generate thrust. Modulators regulate energy ﬂux within a single domain; representative examples include fuel selector valves and
electric bus power control units. Energy Sources encompass storage
systems for one or multiple energy forms, including conventional aviation fuels such as Jet-A and JP-8, compressed or cryogenic fuels such
as liquid petroleum gas (LPG) and liquid hydrogen (LH2 ), and electrochemical storage systems (batteries). Finally, Distributors govern the
transfer of mass, energy, or power among multiple components within
the propulsion architecture. Table 2 summarizes the components currently available for network integration.
By sequentially linking the outputs of one element to the inputs of
the succeeding element, these models can be used to emulate an engine
cycle under quasi-steady, one-dimensional ﬂow assumptions. Ducts, ram
inlets, compression and expansion nozzles, fans, compressors, and turbines form the building blocks of polytropic models for propulsors such
as turbofans, turbojets, turboprops, and internal combustion engines
based on [80,81]. Comparably, electric-rotor and electric-ducted-fan
propulsors consist of an electronic speed controller, an inverter, a motor,
and a rotor or ducted fan.
Understanding thermal sources, losses, and sinks throughout the
system is crucial for several reasons, including improving system eﬃciency by employing a recuperator, regulating the temperatures of other
components, and estimating component failure rates. Some elements
must reject excessive heat loads to prevent thermal runaway and material failure at high temperatures. Waste heat can also be leveraged
for environmental control, as in commercial aircraft, where engine exhaust is used to maintain cabin temperature. Similar to fuel and electrical lines that transport fuel and electrical current, respectively, to
the propulsors, coolant lines transport thermal energy throughout the
aircraft.
Regarding sources, both conformal tanks, such as those found in aircraft wings, and non-conformal tanks can be modeled to simulate liquid
fuel delivery. Battery modules comprising cells of diﬀerent chemistries
enable users to understand the trade-oﬀs of selecting one cell over another, characterized by distinct energy and power densities and by performance degradation over time.

3.3. Stability analyses
The full equations of motion used to model aircraft ﬂight dynamics
are nonlinear and complex; however, for small deviations, these equations can be linearized, yielding a system of linear diﬀerential equations
with constant coeﬃcients [73,74]. This perturbation, or small disturbance, theory is widely used in practice, oﬀering reliable predictions
for unaccelerated ﬂight and acceptable accuracy even for moderate disturbances [75]. Within this small-disturbance theory approach, further
simpliﬁcations are commonly applied to reduce complexity while retaining suﬃcient accuracy. First, derivatives of symmetric forces and moments with respect to asymmetric motion variables are neglected. Second, derivatives with respect to the rates of change of motion variables
(for example, 𝑢, 𝑣, and 𝑤 velocity) are ignored, except for lift and pitching moment changes due to 𝑢 velocity perturbations. Third, equations
are separated into lateral/directional stability and longitudinal stability
categories. It is further noted that these simpliﬁed equations include the
eﬀect of control surfaces on ﬂight conditions. This enables estimation of
the trim conditions of the rudder, elevator, and ailerons under a variety
of steady-state conditions, including cruise, climb, turns, descent, and
others. The stability perturbation method consists of two preprocessing
steps. First, perturbation training data at an equilibrium condition are
obtained by varying the angle of attack, Mach number, velocity, pitch
rate, and control-surface deﬂection to capture stability derivatives. Finally, the stability derivative is computed as the ratio of the diﬀerence
between two stability coeﬃcients to the corresponding perturbation input. For instance, the value of the pitching moment coeﬃcient with
respect to elevator deﬂection is computed as:

3.5. Aeroacoustics analyses
Semi-empirical airframe and jet noise prediction
Empirical models for landing gear [95], slats [96], ﬂaps [97], and
engine noise, which itself comes about as a culmination of diﬀerent
sources–fan, core, and jet exhaust – are integrated into the RCAIDE
framework. Leveraging the recent work by NASA, the fan noise is predicted using the model by Krejsa et al. [98] while the core noise is predicted using the method outlined in Stone et al. [99]. The SAE ARP876D
model [38] for jet exhaust is also integrated into the code. In this particular model, the jet is conceptually divided into three source regions:
the primary (core) jet, the secondary (fan) jet, and the mixed jet. The
total jet sound pressure level is the base-10 logarithm of the sum of
the time-mean-square sound pressures from the three aforementioned
components. At this stage, the engine model does not account for turbomachinery and combustion noise sources. Once the near-ﬁeld sound
spectra are computed for each noise source, the noise module propagates
them to the far-ﬁeld desired position by applying corrections, such as
atmospheric attenuation.

4.2. Solver numerics: A pseudo-spectral approach to solving ﬂight
mechanics
The code employs a pseudospectral collocation method using Chebyshev polynomials, departing from traditional time-marching techniques
for solving the aircraft’s state variables. This method is well-suited to
general boundary-value problems and suﬃciently adaptable to ensure
robustness across any well-posed set of governing equations and boundary (or initial) conditions. Pseudospectral methods oﬀer signiﬁcant advantages over time-marching techniques in problems with multiple design variables, achieving high precision and spectral convergence with
a minimal number of control points. The pseudospectral collocation approach of RCAIDE relies on the formulation of generic diﬀerentiation
D and integration I matrices for a speciﬁed number of control points,
𝑁, within a mission segment. The creation of D and I transpires exclusively during a preprocessing step, incurring minimal computational
cost. Optimal accuracy and convergence rates are achieved when Chebyshev polynomials are used as basis functions with cosine-spaced control points. Diﬀerentiation in physical units is denoted as 𝐷∕(𝑥𝑓 − 𝑥0 ),
whereas integration is denoted as 𝐼 ⋅ (𝑥𝑓 − 𝑥0 ). In this case, 𝑥𝑓 and 𝑥0
denote the ﬁnal and initial values of the independent variable, respectively. Two algorithms from SciPy [104] may be utilized, depending on
the system of equations. The ﬁrst is fsolve, a nonlinear root-ﬁnding algorithm that functions as a wrapper for MINPACK’s “hybrid” method,
executed in Fortran. The second algorithm is Sequential Least Squares
Programming (SLSQP). If the system of equations is square (i.e., N ×
N), both approaches are suitable. Nevertheless, when the number of unknowns exceeds the number of equations, resolution is conﬁned to the

4.3. A novel approach guaranteeing closure of sub-system coupling
interactions
As previously stated, with the increasing adoption of alternative energy systems that fundamentally diﬀer from conventional combustion
systems, secondary components such as distribution lines, pumps, heat
exchangers, and tanks-particularly in cryogenic engines – have become
signiﬁcant bottlenecks in many aircraft designs. These secondary elements frequently consume power or introduce losses, necessitating a
comprehensive plant balance. Although many aircraft synthesis tools
eﬀectively address ﬂight dynamics, their frameworks for assessing powertrain performance are typically open-loop, as shown in Fig. 3a, thereby
inherently ignoring vital interactions that capture unaccounted-for system losses. To address this limitation, two major modiﬁcations have
been implemented. First, additional coupling equations representing
various forms of energy – electrical, thermal, mechanical, pneumatic,
and hydraulic – are incorporated. Second, the framework enables dynamic allocation of energy of a given form; for example, electrical energy can be split between diﬀerent sources, such as a turbofan-driven

Although Sections 3 and 6 provide a comprehensive overview of
capabilities, the solver’s numerical approach restricts the tool to design and analysis applications. The use of a pseudospectral collocation
method for solving time-dependent problems makes the tool particularly
eﬀective for optimal trajectory problems, where quasi-steady solutions
for control variables, such as throttle settings and control surface deﬂections, are computed simultaneously for a speciﬁed maneuver. However, the shift away from ordinary diﬀerential equation (ODE) numerical integration algorithms for continuous-state computation suggests
that RCAIDE is not suitable for the conceptualization, analysis, or optimization of ﬂight controller logic, or for the design of other subsystem
hardware controllers. These tasks require time-marching, a numerical
technique that advances the solution of an initial-value problem from
a known state at time 𝑡 to a future state at 𝑡 + Δ𝑡. Such time-marching
methods advance simulation time using either ﬁxed or variable steps,
balancing accuracy and computational speed according to system dynamics and error tolerances.
In addition to this foundational diﬀerence in the numerical approach, RCAIDE’s mission solvers currently encounter challenges when
handling large, sparse matrices, such as those arising from a 6-DOF
trimmed transition condition for eVTOL vehicles, where each axis involves RPM, pitch command, and thrust vectoring. Ongoing eﬀorts aim
to improve the mission solver’s robustness by fully incorporating analytic gradients through automatic diﬀerentiation. Future analytical developments will focus on advancing high-ﬁdelity aerodynamic models, aerostructural coupling, and aeroelasticity. Furthermore, the implementation of physics-based electrochemical battery models is planned
to capture electrochemical and thermal dynamics with greater accuracy than existing chemistry-based lookup tables. Beyond these speciﬁc
analysis domains, current development includes integrating quantitative
methods for estimating reliability and fault probability. Collectively,
these enhancements are expected to improve the physical ﬁdelity, computational robustness, and design credibility of the simulation environment for next-generation aircraft concepts.
