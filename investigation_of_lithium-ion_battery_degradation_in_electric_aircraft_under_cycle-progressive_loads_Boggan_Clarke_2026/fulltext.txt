Investigation of Lithium-Ion Battery Degradation in
Electric Aircraft Under Cycle-Progressive Loads
Cade Boggan , Graduate Student Member, IEEE, and Matthew A. Clarke , Member, IEEE
Abstract—Electrification is a key step toward reducing aviation anthropogenic emissions; however, significant uncertainties
regarding the long-term degradation behavior of electrochemical
energy storage systems in electric aircraft applications still
remain. Conventional degradation testing often relies on static
load profiles that fail to capture the relationship between cell
aging and time-varying power demands. To address this gap, this
study investigates cycle-progressive load profiles in which system
power demand is incrementally increased to reflect the escalating
electrical requirements imposed by aging batteries on active
thermal management systems. Molicel P30B lithium-ion cells are
subjected to mission-representative electrical load profiles generated using the Research Community Aircraft Interdisciplinary
Design Environment (RCAIDE) computational framework for
an electric conventional takeoff and landing and an electric
vertical takeoff and landing aircraft. Experimental results show
that cycle-progressive profiles accelerate capacity fade relative
to static profiles, exhibiting up to 22.1% greater state-of-health
degradation and distinct shifts in incremental capacity signatures. These findings reveal the need for testing protocols that
incorporate realistic coupled aging-power-demand interactions,
thereby enabling a more accurate performance assessment of
battery systems for electric aircraft applications.
Index Terms—Batteries, degradation, electric aircraft, electric
vehicles, load profile.

I. I NTRODUCTION
LECTRIC aviation is an emerging market as a potential competitor to conventional aircraft for short-haul
passenger and cargo missions [2], [3]. However, due to the
novelty of this transportation mode, the long-term effects of
electric aircraft operation on their batteries remain largely
unknown. Battery degradation testing is a critical aspect of
understanding the lifespan and performance of these vehicles.
This process involves subjecting batteries to controlled cycling
and diagnostic protocols that simulate actual usage, enabling
cell characterization and the evaluation of application-specific
internal aging mechanisms. Such testing provides valuable
insights into how batteries would degrade under real-world
loads, ensuring reliability and accurately predicting longevity.
However, existing testing protocols [4], [5], [6] often apply
static or idealized load profiles that fail to capture the evolving
power demands imposed by degradation. In reality, electric
aircraft that incorporate active battery thermal management

systems (BTMSs) experience a feedback loop between battery
aging and onboard power consumption. As cells degrade,
their internal resistance (IR) increases, leading to greater heat
generation during operation. This, in turn, compels the BTMS
to consume more power to maintain safe battery temperatures,
further increasing the power demand and thus accelerating
cell degradation. Consequently, battery testing that ignores the
relationships between degradation and system power demand
may misrepresent actual degradation rates, leading to inaccurate predictions of range, safety margins, or mission feasibility.
To address this gap, this work introduces a cycle-progressive
load profile—an evolving load protocol designed to better
reflect the system-level consequences of battery aging. The
cycle-progressive load profile modifies the initial baseline
power demand by progressively increasing the load over time,
mimicking the rising loads experienced in electric vehicles
with active thermal management. This increase is driven by
well-known degradation mechanisms such as lithium plating,
particle fracture, solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) growth,
loss of lithium inventory (LLI), and loss of active material
(LAM), all of which contribute to increased IR and heightened
heat generation [7], [8], [9]. As the battery is cycled, this
compounding thermal behavior results in higher BTMS power
requirements, which are directly incorporated into the cycleprogressive load profile. This article aims to serve as an
introduction to the incorporation of a cycle-progressive load
profile for electric aircraft with an active BTMS in laboratory
settings, improving reliability assessment, guiding systemlevel design decisions, and hopefully supporting more robust
certification pathways.
The remainder of this article is organized as follows.
Section II further discusses the theoretical basis for the cycleprogressive load profile. Section III outlines the experimental
design, including the generation of electric aircraft load profiles and the processes for collecting battery degradation data.
Section IV presents and discusses key findings from the
experiment by inspecting capacity and incremental capacity
data. Finally, Section V summarizes the completed work,
highlights potential areas for future investigation, and explores
broader implications for related fields.

Following the general premise behind the cycle-progressive

specificity to this experiment. Although exact measurements
specific to the experimental setup to be detailed in Section III
are not available in the literature, best efforts were made to
link similar data.

However, experimentally, this process cannot be automated

this aircraft and BTMS configuration. Decreases in thermal
management efficiency may also contribute to the value of
incorporating the cycle-progressive load profile for laboratory
tests, but this factor lies outside the scope of this study.
The following three assertions are made to account for
some of the major assumptions in the application of the cycleprogressive load profile.
Assertion 1: The actual heat production of a battery is more
complex than demonstrated above. These complexities include, but are not limited to, the
linearization of IR and the use of the Bernardi
equation for heat generation. Future work should
place emphasis on precise heat generation simulation to improve experimental validity [17].
Assertion 2: Final values will vary depending on the system
at hand, as no two are identical.
Assertion 3: Empirical relations were obtained from data from
similar but not identical testing conditions.
However, for the purposes of introducing the cycleprogressive load profile to the literature, such limitations are
accepted as baseline assumptions but kept in mind during data
assessment.
III. E XPERIMENT M ETHODOLOGY
A. Load Profile Derivation and Generation
The proprietary nature of commercial aviation presents
significant challenges in acquiring empirical electric aircraft
flight data. In the absence of industrial partnerships that would
grant access to such information, research institutions must
employ alternative methodologies to generate representative
load profiles. Conventional approaches often rely on usercreated flight profiles based on reported data concerning motor
efficiency, aircraft mass, and auxiliary unit power demands or
on estimations derived from expected operational parameters.
However, these methodologies are inherently limited due to
their reliance on various simplifications and the omission of
the coupled influences of these systems. This limitation is
particularly pronounced in the modeling of power sources,
where crucial factors such as battery temperature dynamics,
electrochemical characteristics, and thermal management system efficiency are frequently overlooked or oversimplified.
To address these limitations and enhance the fidelity of
the modeling of electric aircraft performance, this study
employs an advanced computational aircraft modeling framework: the Research Community Aircraft Interdisciplinary
Design Environment (RCAIDE) [18]. RCAIDE is a state-ofthe-art, open-source, Python-based platform that allows for

For this investigation, two aircraft architectures were chosen: an electric conventional take-off and landing (eCTOL)
aircraft (inspired by the NASA X-57 Maxwell) and a tiltwing electric vertical take-off and landing (eVTOL) aircraft
(inspired by the Airbus A3 Vahana demonstrator). These two
aircraft were selected to explore the impact of the cycleprogressive load profile on drastically different configurations.
To isolate the impact of aircraft configuration and mitigate
potential confounding factors, the discharge throughput of
each cell is kept constant across both base flight profiles. This
was achieved by reducing the cruise duration of the eCTOL
aircraft, in turn affecting the overall flight range and power
requirements. It should be noted that while this holds true
for the initial power profiles, subsequent variations in battery
degradation may impact the equivalency of charge throughput
across aircraft configurations.

A general overview of the experimental data collection procedure is described as follows. In short, cells are preprocessed
and characterized, subjected to their respective simulated flight
loads, and then the final state is again characterized. An initial
cell conditioning step was performed to identify outliers and
to ensure consistent measurement conditions for subsequent
characterization [22]. As cells used in the experiment are of
commercial grade, this conditioning step is not to be confused
with SEI/CEI formation conditioning cycles. For the conditioning step, each cell underwent three full charge–discharge
cycles at 1C. This aims to reduce variability between cells
in the subsequent capacity measurement. It should be noted
that during the conditioning step, two outlying batteries were
observed (cells 10 and 18 having initial capacities below the
manufacturer-specified 2.9-Ah minimum capacity), resulting
in a single cell being used for analysis of the CCP profile.
Following this was the characterization of the initial cell
state. Incremental capacity analysis (ICA) was employed as a

noninvasive diagnostic tool to monitor changes in the internal
state of the battery over time. ICA can provide insight into
LAM, LLI, and increases in IR through the analysis of
differential capacity (dQ/dV) curves [23]. For ICA, dQ/dV
values were calculated in the native software of the battery
cycler, requiring no additional smoothing. For the ICA tests,
cells were charged using a constant current-constant voltage
(CCCV) protocol. The constant current phase was performed
at a low C-rate (C/20) and continued to a voltage of 4.2 V.
This was followed by a constant voltage phase until the
current dropped to C/30. A 20-h rest period followed to ensure
thermal and electrochemical stabilization before discharging
the cell. Cells were discharged at a constant current (C/20)
until reaching the terminal voltage of 2.5 V. A low C-rate
for both charging and discharging was chosen to maximize
measurement accuracy, as higher rates can result in poor
analysis of dQ/dV curves as a result of subtle changes in
cell behavior being missed. The selection of discharge curves
as the primary segment of reference is due to the interest
in the discharging characteristics of the battery for in-flight
operation.
The primary driver of battery degradation in this study was
the application of the simulated flight cycles in the form of
electrical load profiles. The flight cycle procedure consists of
three stages: a charging segment, a 5-min rest period, and
the flight profile segment. Cells were charged using a CCCV
protocol at 1C until the cell reached a voltage of 4.2 V,
followed by a constant voltage phase until the current dropped
to C/30.

conducted every 100 flight cycles to assess the capacity at

followed the same protocol as the ICA test, but with a reduced
rest period of 1 h. Normalized capacity Q̃n in this study is
defined as the ratio of the measured discharge capacity at a

IV. D EGRADATION R ESULTS
A. State-of-Health Observations
Fig. 4 depicts the capacity decrease over 1000 flight cycles.
Overall, similar general trends are observed, but key subtleties
emerge that indicate different degradation modes and rates in
the control and experimental groups. At the inception of the
experiments, there is only a minor variation in capacity loss
between batteries under fixed versus cycle-progressive load
profiles. This can be attributed to the similar power draw
initially between the two profile types. Notably, it is observed
that the baseline load profiles for both aircraft architectures
exhibit a lower capacity drop than the compressed profiles.
This can likely be attributed to the increased C-rate cycling.
In some tests, a slight increase in capacity was observed at
the beginning of the experiment, particularly for the fixed
and cycle-progressive baseline eVTOL load profiles. From
Guo et al. [24], this observation could be explained through
electrochemical activation.

As the number of flight cycles increases, the rate of degradation between the cycle-progressive load profile experiments
and fixed profile experiments remains essentially the same.
It is only around the 500–600 cycle mark that the increased
power draw becomes visually evident between the two profile
types. Depicted in Table IV are the total average decreases in
the capacity for the eight experiment types.
Across the board, greater decreases in battery capacity
were observed from profiles incorporating a cycle-progressive
profile. This much should be evident from the increased
discharge throughput and peak discharge power. For the baseline power eCTOL, a 9.1% relative increase in capacity loss
was observed; for the compressed eCTOL, a 13.3% relative
increase in capacity loss was observed; for the baseline power

While three cells are not enough to fully account for cell-tocell variability, they do provide greater credibility toward the
observed results stemming from the profile in question. This
is in comparison to commonly conducted testing protocols
that only use one cell per variable of interest. Further work
incorporating a greater number of cells would increase the
statistical significance of experiments incorporating cycleprogressive load profiles. In comparison to other literature
revolving around lithium-ion battery degradation in electric
aircraft, low levels of degradation are observed relative to
the number of flights tested. A multitude of variables can
contribute to this result, with the leading theory relating
to the relatively low depth of discharge. Higher levels of
depth of discharge can lead to significantly lower levels
of cycle life retention [25]. Moderate ambient temperature
[26], resting times in between cycles, and greater cycling
dynamics [27] (as in this experiment) can improve capacity
retention.

In addition, this study represents the first report of restinduced regeneration for electric aircraft applications. After
500 flight cycles, cells were subjected to rest periods exceeding
360 h, simulating long-duration periods of inactivity such
as maintenance. Capacity recovery was observed in multiple
cells, with the average rate of capacity fade following the
rest period decreasing across the board. This is consistent
with previous literature for other applications of lithium-ion
batteries [28], [29], [30], [31]. While further investigation
is necessary, this finding introduces an additional dimension
of realism for laboratory electric aircraft battery degradation
testing.

exhibit near-perfect alignment across the entire voltage range.

While the sample size for certain groups, such as the CCP

As expected, LLI and LAM are the dominant degradation
mechanisms during the first 1000 cycles. This is evident
from the shifts in peaks between 3.5 and 4.1 V, which align
with prior studies suggesting that cathode-side degradation is
initially the primary driver. Interestingly, the cycle-progressive
groups exhibit a distinct increase in peak magnitude and
a shift in morphology within the 3.15–3.5-V region that is
not observed in the static profiles, even those with higher
C-rates (baseline/compressed eVTOL). This suggests that the
“progressive” nature of the load, which mimics the escalating
thermal management power demands of an aging system,
triggers different electrochemical staging in the graphite anode
than a fixed high-power mission. Further work to identify the
root cause will assist greatly in the wider understanding of the
impact of cycle-progressive profiles for electric aircraft battery
testing. Specifically, the growth in the dQ/dV features in this
lower voltage range may indicate altered lithium intercalation
kinetics or accelerated SEI growth specific to the evolving load
profile.
In this study, ICA is intended primarily as a qualitative
diagnostic tool to highlight these shifts that standard SOH
capacity fade metrics may overlook. Higher energy density
batteries are a necessity for powering electric aircraft, and
design choices must be made to mitigate the degradation
variation exhibited by cycle-progressive load profiles.
V. C ONCLUSION
This study investigated the influence of cycle-progressive
load profiles on lithium-ion battery degradation in electric
aircraft, focusing on the coupled feedback between cell aging
and active BTMSs. Using eCTOL and eVTOL load profiles
derived from the RCAIDE framework, we subjected commercial Molicel P30B cells to fixed and cycle-progressive
load profiles over 1000 flight cycles. Across all test cases,
the cycle-progressive load profiles consistently resulted in
greater degradation than their fixed counterparts (up to 22.1%
relative increase in degradation). While initial capacity fade
remains similar between the two profile types, the ICA curves
reveal that the cycle-progressive protocol eventually alters the
degradation pathways. Specifically, the deviation in peak shape
and magnitude within the 3.15–3.5-V range, not observed in
static profiles even at higher C-rates, indicates that progressive
loading triggers unique anode-side staging and kinetics, likely
associated with accelerated SEI growth and LLI.
The findings emphasize that the cycle-progressive profile
more realistically captures the system-level consequences of
battery aging in aircraft with active cooling. While the limited

cell count necessitated by the resource-intensive nature of
flight-cycle testing prevents claims of broad statistical significance, the near-perfect alignment of dQ/dV signatures at Cycle
0 provides strong evidence that the observed mechanistic
shifts are profile-induced. Future work utilizing electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and postmortem analysis will be
essential to further quantify these evolving degradation modes.
The authors aim for this work to ultimately build greater
confidence in the viability of battery-powered electric aviation.
