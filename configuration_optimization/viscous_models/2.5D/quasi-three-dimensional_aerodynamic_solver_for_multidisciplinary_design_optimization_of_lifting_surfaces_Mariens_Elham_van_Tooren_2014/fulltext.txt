This paper presents the development of a quasi-three-dimensional aerodynamic solver, which provides accurate
results for wing drag comparable to the higher-fidelity aerodynamic solvers at significantly lower computational
costs. The proposed solver calculates the viscous wing drag using the combination of a two-dimensional airfoil
analysis tool with a vortex lattice code. Validation results show that the results of the quasi-three-dimensional solver
are in good agreement with higher-fidelity computational fluid dynamics solvers. The quasi-three-dimensional solver
is used for a wing shape multidisciplinary design optimization. A multidisciplinary design optimization problem
is formulated to design the wing shape of a typical passenger aircraft. The aircraft maximum takeoff weight is
considered as the objective function. Two optimization algorithms, a local and a global optimum finder, are
implemented in the multidisciplinary design optimization system. The optimization results indicate that the global
optimization algorithm shows a slightly greater reduction in maximum takeoff weight. However, finding the global
optimum needs about 20 times the computational time of the local optimization algorithm.

The calculation of the wing primary aerodynamic forces, especially drag, is an important element in early design stages of an
aircraft. Higher-fidelity aerodynamic solvers offer, in general, better
approximations of the actual flow around the wing and thus more
accurate results. However, the use of these solvers results in high
computational cost. Lower-fidelity aerodynamic solvers use more
simplified models resulting in faster turnaround times but less
accurate results.
Optimization of an aircraft wing requires many repetitive aerodynamic evaluations of different wing shapes. Using high-fidelity
aerodynamic solvers in optimization leads to extensive computing
costs, whereas lower-fidelity solvers are significantly faster but
provide less accurate results. Therefore, it is of great interest to find a
fast way to calculate the primary aerodynamic forces with sufficient
accuracy. An interesting way for establishing such an approach is by
combining (two-dimensional) viscous airfoil data with an inviscid
three-dimensional wing lift calculation.
Various applications of this approach have been done in the past
[4–8]. Most applications calculate the wing lift using a lifting-line
method or a vortex lattice method (VLM). The three-dimensional
wing lift distribution can be combined with experimental viscous
airfoil data to predict the total wing drag [4,5]. The range of
applicability can be extended by using viscous airfoil calculation
tools instead of experimental data [8]. The viscous airfoil data can
also be predicted using empirical equations [6].
The aforementioned applications are not able to fulfill all the needs
of a designer, who would like to have a tool that is fast, accurate,
design sensitive, suitable to support optimization, and can evaluate a
wide range of different wing shapes. For instance, using experimental
airfoil data limits the application of the aerodynamic solvers only for
wings, which have airfoils with available experimental data. These
solvers cannot be applied in a wing optimization, where the wing
airfoil shape is a design variable. Using empirical equations is a way
to increase the applicability of the solvers; however, it comes at a
reduced level of accuracy. Using an airfoil analysis tool as presented
by Hileman et al. [8] is a way to improve the applicability of this kind
of aerodynamic solver; however, the method presented in [8] uses a

II.

Quasi-Three-Dimensional Aerodynamic Solver

The total drag of a wing is decomposed into three components:
profile drag, induced drag, and wave drag. The profile drag, which is
associated with the airfoil shape, is the sum of the form drag and the
friction drag. The induced drag is the drag due to the lift generated
vorticity shed into the wake. This is highly affected by the wing
spanwise lift distribution and it is minimum for the elliptical lift
distribution. The wave drag is the drag due to generation of the shock
waves at high speed.
In the proposed method, the wing induced drag is calculated using
the Trefftz plane analysis [9], whereas the profile drag and the wave
drag are calculated using the strip theory [10,11]. In this approach, the
wing geometry is divided into several two-dimensional (2-D)
spanwise wing sections, and the aerodynamic forces and moments on
each segment are calculated using the effective velocity and angle of
attack. The overall procedure of the proposed method is broken down
into the following three stages: 1) calculation of the wing lift
distribution, the wing lift coefficient CL , and the wing induced drag
coefficient CDi for a given wing geometry and flow condition, using a
VLM; 2) calculation of the profile and wave drag of the 2-D sections
by means of an airfoil analysis tool, applying the strip theory and
using the outputs of stage 1; and 3) calculation of the wing total drag.
These steps are explained in the following sections.

A. Method Description
1. Stage One

A VLM is used to calculate the spanwise lift distribution of the
wing for a given angle of attack α or a given lift coefficient CL .
Furthermore, the VLM code also calculates the wing induced drag
coefficient using the Trefftz plane analysis.
2. Stage Two

In this stage, the strip theory is extended using simple sweep theory
[12] to calculate the aerodynamic characteristics of the 2-D wing
sections (airfoil shape) at several spanwise locations.
The following steps are used to determine drag coefficients at each
wing section:
1) Interpolation on the three-dimensional lift distribution of the
wing (calculated by the VLM) to find the local lift coefficient Cl at the

2) VGK [23] uses a finite difference solution of the full potential
equations integrated with the integral method for the boundary layer
(both laminar and turbulent) to calculate pressure and friction drag of
an airfoil [24].
3) MSES [25] is an interactive viscous/inviscid Euler method that
features the design and analysis of single and multi-element airfoils at
low Reynolds numbers and transonic Mach numbers. In addition,
MSES can also predict flows with transitional separation bubbles,
shock waves, trailing edge, and shock-induced separations [26].
At subsonic regime, the aerodynamic solver uses XFOIL for airfoil
analysis. XFOIL shows accurate results in low speed conditions
and it is the fasted tool among the listed solvers. For transonic flows,
the Q3D solver uses VGK or MSES. As mentioned before, VGK
uses full potential equation. The solutions of the full potential
equation are exact for shock-free flows, but are only approximate for
flows with shocks of appreciable strength because the regions across
the shock are rotational (and therefore nonisentropic). For a good
representation of such flows, solutions of the Euler equations are
required (note that, with a suitable choice of several parameters [23],
the solutions of VGK yield surface pressure distributions that
correspond closely to those of true physical shock waves). MSES
uses Euler equations; therefore, it provides more accurate results than
VGK in the case of strong shock. However, using MSES results in
higher computational time.
C. Selection of Panel Density and Number of Sections

The panel density (in VLM) and the number of sections (for strip
method) should be selected properly. The panel density has an

influence on the calculation of the induced drag, whereas the number
of wing sections affects the profile drag prediction.
1. VLM Panel Density

A vortex lattice code requires a panel density as a user input. The
panel density for the Q3D solver is derived for a converged solution
(312 panels are sufficient, 24 spanwise and 13 chordwise). Note
that the panel density only affects the induced drag calculation. The
higher the number of panels, the more the computational time
increases. The derived panel density has only a small deviation on the
calculated induced drag within 0.15% compared to a calculation
using a very high panel density (600 panels).
2. Number of Wing Sections

To select the proper number of two-dimensional wing sections, the
effect of the number of segments on the calculated profile drag
coefficient is plotted in Fig. 5. The value of the profile drag coefficient
is normalized using the average value of the three coefficients
calculated using the highest number of sections. Increasing the
number of sections linearly increases the computational time;
therefore, eight wing sections were selected, implying a deviation of
the calculated profile drag coefficient smaller than 0.01% compared
to the highest number of sections.
D. Validation

The Q3D aerodynamic solver is validated for both subsonic and
transonic regimes. To validate the solver, a higher-fidelity aerodynamic solver called MATRICS-V [27] is used. The MATRICS-V
flow solver is based on fully conservative full potential outer flow
in quasi-simultaneous interaction with an integral boundary layer
method on the wing. The code can be used to treat fixed angle of
attack as well as fixed lift analysis problems, and features: 1) threedimensional laminar and turbulent boundary layers; 2) two options
for the laminar to turbulent boundary layer transition; 3) capability
to model considerable boundary layer separation; 4) shock capturing
for supersonic/subsonic shock waves consistent with Rankine–
Hugoniot relations; and 5) multigrid acceleration of flow solver.
The MATRICS-V flow solver has been validated using wind
tunnel data and flight test data. Figures 6 and 7 from [27] show the
results of the MATRICS-V compared to the wind tunnel and flight
test results for Fokker 100 wing/body configuration. The wind tunnel
test (Fig. 6) has been performed at M∞  0.779, α  1.03 deg, and
Re∞  3 × 106 .The results shown in Fig. 7 are for M∞  0.775,
α  1.0 deg, and Re∞  35 × 106 . As can be concluded from
Figs. 6 and 7, MATRICS-V provides results that are in good
agreement with experimental and in-flight test data.

Wing Shape Multidisciplinary Design Optimization

The proposed Q3D aerodynamic solver has been used for a series
of wing shape multidisciplinary design optimizations. A typical
mid-range passenger aircraft is used as the test case. The MDO
architecture is configured in a modular way and the analysis contains
various subroutines representing different design disciplines. The
analysis modules include aerodynamics, weight, and performance.
Two optimization algorithms were used: the well-known sequential
quadratic programming (SQP) algorithm and the local optima
smoothing (LOS) algorithm for global optimization (see Sec. III.C).

Two optimization algorithms are used in the MDO system. The
SQP algorithm has been shown to work effectively for large,
nonlinear problems in engineering applications [35]. LOS is an
algorithm for global exploration that tries to guide random
exploration toward the region of attraction [36]. This algorithm is
briefly explained here.
The main idea behind the LOS algorithm is originated by applying
smoothing techniques to noisy functions. See [36,37] for more
detailed information. LOS works as follows:
1) Find the local optimum, starting from an initial point. Set this
optimum as the current optimum.
2) Perform local searches starting from K uniform sampled points
inside a sphere (with radius r) around the current optimum. Each time
a new optimum is found, the sampling procedure restarts around the
new optimum. At the point where no new optimum is found, the
algorithm proceeds to the next step.
3) A smoothed (piecewise-constant) function is constructed using
the local optima of the K observed samples. Consequently, a local
search is performed on the smoothed function. The local optima
found from the smoothed function is then used as the starting point to

Optimization Results

Table 9 summarizes the results of the optimizations using both
SQP and LOS algorithms. The optimized planforms using both
algorithms are depicted in Fig. 19. From Table 9, it can be observed
that the LOS algorithm shows a slightly higher reduction in the
aircraft MTOW compared to the SQP algorithm. However, finding
this greater reduction comes at the cost of increased computational
time. The optimization using the SQP algorithm required 14.5 h,
whereas the LOS algorithm required 331 h (on a computer having an
Intel Core2Duo E4400 (2.00 Ghz), with 2 Gb RAM).

V.

developed to analyze wing aerodynamics in a rapid way while
having high accuracy. This solver was validated using results of a
higher-fidelity CFD code called MATRICS-V for a typical turboprop
and a typical turbofan passenger aircraft. The validation of the Q3D
solver showed that the results are within 2% error compared to the

The Q3D solver has been used in a wing optimization task. The

quadratic programming (SQP) algorithm and the local optima

global optimum in such an optimization task comes at the cost
of longer computational times. For this optimization problem, the
LOS algorithm requires about 22 times more computational time
compared to SQP to find a slightly better optimum (the amount of
reduction in MTOW achieved by LOS is less than 0.5% higher than
SQP). Therefore, it can be concluded that the SQP algorithm is a
better choice for such wing shape optimizations in the conceptual
design phase.
