The Squire–Young formula is widely used to calculate profile drag in two-dimensional airfoil analyses. Although
experience shows that the formula provides reasonable and accurate predictions, full Navier–Stokes computational
fluid dynamics allows for a numerical validation of the formula. A method is presented for evaluating the Squire–
Young formula from a computational fluid dynamics solution, and demonstrated for several airfoils. The agreement
between profile-drag coefficients determined using the Squire–Young formula and those calculated directly from the
computational fluid dynamics solution using surface integration is typically within 2–3% in the low-drag range, with
error increasing at higher lift coefficients. The applicability of the underlying assumptions is discussed, and the
validity of the assumed variation of shape factor and edge velocity in the wake is investigated for a symmetric airfoil at
zero lift. The formula is also demonstrated as being insensitive to spurious drag that may occur due to far-field
boundary conditions. Finally, a derivation of the Squire–Young formula is included to further aid in understanding
the influence of its assumptions on its predictions.

The additional quantities present in Eq. (6), as compared to Eq. (5),

the limit of incompressibility (M∞ → 0), Eq. (6) reduces to the
classic, incompressible form of the Squire–Young formula [Eq. (5)].
The formulae presented in Eqs. (5) and (6) are extrapolations of the
momentum deficit at the trailing edge of an airfoil to the deficit that
would exist in a wake far downstream. (Additional details of this,
including the applicable assumptions, are described in the
Appendix.) The extrapolated quantities are intended to represent the
drag that would be calculated with a true control-volume momentum
balance around an airfoil, and, as such, the calculated drag includes
both the skin friction and any total-pressure losses in the boundary
layer.
In a full Navier–Stokes computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
solution, such extrapolations are often regarded as being unnecessary
because the entire flowfield is resolved without the use of boundarylayer approximations or any other decoupling of the viscous and
inviscid regions. The wall shear stresses, as well as all boundary-layer
pressure losses, are realized at the airfoil surface with sufficient
resolution to directly integrate the total drag. Nevertheless, it is still
possible to estimate the drag based on computations away from the
body, such as in the wake-integration technique demonstrated by
Chao and van Dam [6]. Their work used a control-volume analysis
with the actual far-downstream wake to integrate the drag, in which a
reasonably good agreement with experiment and surface integration
was found.
The present study follows a similar tone, except that the focus here
is on the accuracy of the Squire–Young formula for predicting the
profile drag of low-speed airfoils. As a primary means of investigating this, the section aerodynamic characteristics for several
airfoils at varying Reynolds numbers are predicted using Reynoldsaveraged Navier–Stokes (RANS) CFD, and the profile drags are also
calculated by applying the Squire–Young formula to the same CFD
solutions. Both fully turbulent and transitional solutions are used to
assess how the choice of model influences the physics of the flowfield
and the accuracy of the predictions, although it must be noted that the
solutions are affected by the limitations of the turbulence modeling.
Additionally, the validity of the shape-factor/velocity relationship
used to derive the Squire–Young formula is assessed and discussed.
Finally, the ability of the Squire–Young formula to filter out spurious
drag due to finite domain size is demonstrated.

II.

Application of the Formula to CFD Solutions

The challenge in applying the Squire–Young formula to a full
RANS solution for an external aerodynamic flow is that there is no
explicit distinction between the boundary-layer region and the outer
flow. Instead, the Navier–Stokes equations are solved for the entire
flowfield with the influence of viscosity introduced by the presence
of no-slip boundary conditions and nonzero viscosity. While the
outer flow is typically characterized as being inviscid and irrotational,
this is actually an asymptotic behavior, and the interface between
it and the boundary layer is somewhat ill-defined. (For example,
Blasius defined the “edge” of the boundary layer to occur when the
inner solution is 99% of the outer solution.) Thus, it can be difficult to
characterize a specific boundary-layer height and edge velocity when
evaluating Eqs. (3) and (4). Instead, the analysis can make use of
Prandtl’s boundary-layer approximations, which assume that the
pressure gradient normal to the wall in the boundary layer is
negligible. From this assumption and the assumption that the total
enthalpy remains constant in the absence of viscosity, an equivalent

inviscid flowfield based on the actual pressure field can now be
defined. For a calorically perfect ideal gas, this flowfield can be
described using the compressible Bernoulli equation, given by

III.

Comparison of Squire–Young and
Surface-Integrated Drags

The approach outlined in Sec. II was applied to predictions made
using the OVERFLOW 2.2f solver [7]. Five airfoils were analyzed:
the Pennsylvania State University (PSU) 94-097 winglet airfoil [8],
the DAE 11 human-powered-aircraft airfoil [9,10], the Somers S824
symmetric wind-turbine airfoil [11,12], the Somers S805 windturbine airfoil [13,14], and the NASA NLF(1)-0416 general-aviation
airfoil [15]. These airfoils are shown in Fig. 1. The Reynolds numbers
considered for these airfoils range from 0.24 × 106 to 4.0 × 106 .
Although it is possible to analyze a single airfoil across this
Reynolds-number range, doing so would inhibit the ability to
understand the general behavior of the Squire–Young formula across
different geometries. Experimental profile-drag measurements are
available for these airfoils; however, those data are not included here
because comparing CFD predictions to experiment is not the focus of
this study.

using Chimera Grid Tools [18] following best practices in grid

Wake Shape Factor and Velocity

One of the main assumptions involved in the derivation of the
Squire–Young formula is that the shape factor in the wake varies
linearly with the natural logarithm of the edge velocity. The zero-lift
flow solutions for the symmetric S824 airfoil provide a unique opportunity to investigate the validity of this assumption because the wake is
perfectly aligned with the C-cut in the computational mesh, and the grid
clustering in this region facilitates an accurate determination of the
wake properties. This quality does not hold for general flow solutions,
which suffer from inadequate grid resolution in the wake.
The shape factor and edge velocities were determined along the
extent of the wake for both the transitional and fully turbulent S824
zero-lift solutions. These data, plotted in Fig. 7, show a very sharp
decrease in the shape factor immediately downstream of the airfoil
trailing edge before settling into a nearly linear decay. It is unclear,
however, if this behavior is actually representative of the flow physics
or a result of RANS turbulence modeling. In the wake, the shapefactor decay is a function of the decay in the velocity deficit, which, in
turn, is a function of the eddy viscosity in the wake, while the
variation of the edge velocity is largely independent of the viscous
modeling, as it is part of the inviscid outer flow. An additional fully
turbulent zero-lift solution was generated using the Menter k-ω shearstress-transport (SST) eddy-viscosity model [21] that shows the same
qualitative shape-factor behavior as the SA-based solutions. These
data are also plotted in Fig. 7. Seeing this behavior from two different
eddy-viscosity models does not confirm it to be representative of
real flows, but it does show that the behavior is not limited to the
SA model.
Despite the differences between the actual and assumed shapefactor distributions in the wake, numerical integration reveals that this
affects the predicted drag by 0.81 and 0.80% for the transitional and
fully turbulent (SA) solutions, respectively. A unique property of the
assumed velocity/shape-factor relationship in the wake is that it is
equivalent to assuming that the shape factor is constant and equal to
the arithmetic average of the trailing-edge and far-downstream values
[22]. For all three distributions plotted in Fig. 6, the equivalent

coefficients predicted using the Squire–Young formula is that they
are consistently less than the surface-integrated drag coefficients. It
cannot be determined from the comparisons in Sec. III whether this
behavior is a deficiency of the Squire–Young formula or an artifact of
having a finite computational domain in the OVERFLOW simulations. Although the computational grids for all airfoils have far-field
boundaries located 1000 chords from the surface, OVERFLOW lacks

VI.

Conclusions

The use of the Squire–Young formula for estimating profile drag
shows strong agreement with the surface-integration methods for full
Navier–Stokes CFD solutions, with errors being within 2–3% in the
low-drag ranges of the airfoils. The formula tends to be most accurate
when laminar–turbulent transition is included in the CFD solutions.
This is due to a combination of a thinner wake and lower shape factor
at the trailing edge as compared to the fully turbulent solutions, which
mitigates the errors in the underlying assumptions used to derive the
formula. Furthermore, transitional flows are more representative of
the wind-tunnel environment than fully turbulent flows, likely
contributing to the continued practical success of the Squire–Young
formula. The influence of boundary-layer thickness was observed at
higher lift coefficients, even for the transitional solutions, as significant deviations between the Squire–Young formula and the surface
integration occur as the boundary layer nears separation. An unexpected behavior was observed for cases in which a significant
upper-surface trailing-edge separation occurs relatively suddenly, as
the trailing-edge momentum thickness decreases with increasing lift,
and an apparent jump in the profile-drag polar occurs. It must be
emphasized, though, that the assumptions involved with the Squire–
Young formula break down with separated flow.
One of the key assumptions of the formula, which is the
relationship between shape factor and edge velocity in the wake, was
investigated for the special case of a symmetric airfoil at zero lift. It
was found for several different turbulence-closure methods that the
shape factor decreases very abruptly in the near wake before settling
into a near-linear behavior toward the far wake. The difference
between what was assumed in the formula and what was observed in
the CFD solutions biases the average shape factor toward that of the
far wake by approximately 10%, but this affects the predicted profiledrag coefficient by less than 1%.
A useful quality of the Squire–Young drag estimates was demonstrated in that the formula is insensitive to spurious drags introduced
by far-field boundary treatments. The validation cases were run with
far-field boundaries located 1000 chords from the surface, which
mostly mitigates the lack of a vortex correction in the solver and the
accompanying spurious drag. Solutions were generated with closer

boundaries, and while spurious drag became more significant as the
distance decreased, the boundary-layer development was not affected. Accordingly, the Squire–Young formula predicts nearly identical
profile-drag coefficients for all boundary distances.
In the end, it is truly remarkable how accurate the Squire–Young
formula is when one considers how many assumptions were used to
derive it. It was assumed that the momentum-integral equation is
valid at the airfoil trailing edge and that this equation is valid in the
wake. A functional relationship between the velocity and shape factor
in the wake was assumed to enable streamwise integration of
the momentum-integral equation from the trailing edge to the fardownstream wake. Finally, it was assumed that the profile-drag contributions from the upper and lower surfaces can be separately
evaluated and summed. And yet, the Squire–Young formula performs
very well in spite of the assumptions used to derive it.
