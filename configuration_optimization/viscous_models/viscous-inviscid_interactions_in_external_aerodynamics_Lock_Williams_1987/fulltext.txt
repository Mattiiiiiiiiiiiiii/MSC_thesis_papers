Abstract--After a short introduction, Section 2 provides a physical background to the subject of
viscous-inviscid interactions (VII). Section 3 covers the basic theoretical principles of interactive methods
in two dimensions: generalisations of the concept of the displacement effect and the momentum integral
equation; the effect of normal pressure gradients; matching conditions in the wake. The boundary
conditions required for the equivalent inviscid flow (EIF) are then summarised, and the section concludes
with a brief discussion of some relevant features of 'integral' and 'differential' methods for turbulent
boundary layers.
In Section 4 a similar, though necessarily incomplete, account is given of the corresponding treatment
in three dimensions. Section 5 describes the principles of techniques for coupling the viscous and inviscid
components of an interactive method; first in general principles and then in greater detail. In Section 6 a
number of recent VII methods are reviewed and compared with experiment: first in two-dimensional
incompressible flow (single and multiple aerofoils); then for aerofoils in transonic flow; an example of axisymmetric transonic flow; and finally methods for three-dimensional wings in transonic flow. The
conclusions briefly summarise the current situation regarding the development of VII methods, and
consider future possibilities in the subject.

1. INTRODUCTION
In the external aerodynamics of aircraft, the ultimate problem is to be able to calculate,
as accurately and quickly as possible, the basic mean flow properties--pressure, velocity,
temperature, skin friction and overall forces, for example---on and near the surface of what
is invariably an extremely complicated shape. Since air is a viscous, compressible fluid, the
equations that we have to solve are those of Navier and Stokes, while at the high Reynolds
numbers involved in aviation large regions of turbulent flow will occur, so that some form
of 'turbulence modelling' is inevitable.
Even with this simplification--which allows us, for flows which are steady in the overall
sense, to treat the steady, time-averaged form of the Navier-Stokes equations--the
computational problems involved are formidable. Nevertheless, remarkable progress is
currently being made in the development of practical 'Navier-Stokes solvers', and the

extension to three dimensions is certainly in sight;~1~ but the over-simplification of the
turbulence modelling that is still usually employed renders the results of limited practical
value, while the computational times involved, even on the latest generation of
'supercomputers', remain prohibitive for routine applications.
Fortunately, however, in many problems of practical importance the effects of viscosity
and turbulence are confined to relatively thin shear layers in the immediate neighbourhood of the 'wetted' surfaces and their wakes; outside these regions the inviscid
Euler equations are applicable to a very high degree of accuracy. It is therefore
advantageous to use the technique known as 'viscous-inviscid interaction' (VII), whereby
separate calculation methods for the external inviscid flow and the viscous shear layers are
combined interactively to provide a composite solution to the problem. This has been a
fruitful approach for many years and has reached an advanced state of development, so
that a review of the subject at the present time is highly appropriate.
In the past, the treatment of the viscous side of the problem has usually involved the
basic assumptions of 'classical' boundary-layer theory: that the pressure variation across
the boundary is negligible and that only normal derivatives of the shear stresses are
important, while for turbulent flows the Reynolds normal stresses are almost invariably
neglected. There are, however, a number of situations of great practical importance-mostly involving flow separation--where these simplifying assumptions are by no means
valid; some of these situations are described qualitatively in Section 2, in which an
account of the physical background to the subject is given. Fortunately, it is possible (at
least in two-dimensional flow) to make allowances--approximate but usually adequate-for most of these 'higher-order' effects in a relatively simple way by considering the overall
integrated properties of the shear layers, as explained in Section 3, in which the rival
merits of this 'integral' approach, as compared with the potentially more accurate, but
usually more time-consuming, 'differential' methods, are briefly discussed. Some
additional features of integral methods, needed to improve their accuracy in separated
flow conditions, are also described.
In particular, we show in Section 3 how a generalisation can be derived of the wellknown 'displacement effect' of the shear layers, which can be used to provide an inner
boundary condition for the outer component of an interactive method, the calculation of
the inviscid flow (usually extended over the region actually occupied by the viscous layers
to form an 'equivalent inviscid flow'). The final step in the process therefore is to organise
the two separate parts--viscous and inviscid--of the overall calculation to interact with
each other in an iterative way so that convergence to the final solution is achieved as
economically and accurately as possible. In recent years, three different approaches to this
aspect of the problem have been identified, known as 'direct', 'semi-inverse' (SI) and 'quasisimultaneous' (QS), and these are explained in some detail in Section 5.
Both the SI and QS techniques have been developed specifically for the treatment of
problems involving boundary-layer separation, and in two-dimensional flows they have
been remarkably successful, as shown in Section 6 in which a number of recent methods of
this type are described and comparisons with experiment given. The crucial extension of
these ideas to general three-dimensional problems is naturally at a much earlier stage,
mainly because of the complexity of the analysis and programming involved. In Section 6
the few recent methods for swept wings that have been published are described and some
results given; and the possibility of extending them to treat substantial boundary-layer
separation is discussed.

3. BASIC THEORETICAL PRINCIPLES OF INTERACTIVE METHODS
Whether a 'differential' or 'integral' method is to be used for calculating the viscous
shear layers, the first step in developing a VII procedure is to derive the appropriate inner
boundary condition that is required for the outer component, the calculation of the
external inviscid flow. This provides the well-known 'displacement effect' of the boundary
layer and wake; and we shall show below how it can be generalised without difficulty to
treat almost all of the 'difficult' situations mentioned in Section 2. Using similar ideas, a
corresponding generalisation can be made of the momentum integral equation that forms
the basis of all 'integral' methods for viscous problems; and a simple approximation can
be derived for the variation of pressure normal to the surface when the streamlines are
highly curved, which should be adequate in many cases of practical interest. Even when a
'differential' method is employed, the streamwise momentum integral equation should still
be useful in checking the overall accuracy of the solution, while the simplified treatment of
the effects of normal pressure gradient could be used (as in Ref. 23, for example) as an
alternative to solving the full normal momentum equation.
The first point to consider is the position of the surface at which the matching between
the inner (viscous) and outer (inviscid) parts of the solution is to be imposed. Perhaps the
most obvious--but not necessarily the best--choice for this is at (or near) the outer edge of
the shear layers, thus avoiding the need for overlapping the two solutions and minimising
the extent of the region that has to be covered by the inviscid calculations. At this surface,
the matching conditions are simply that the velocity fields of the two component parts
should be continuous in magnitude and direction across the interface (see Sketch 6). Just
such a procedure has been used by Bradshaw and his colleagues at Imperial College, 13- 51
who combine in this way what is essentially a solution, with minimum simplification, of
the full (time-averaged) Navier-Stokes equations, with a suitable inviscid solver (panel or
finite-difference) method for the external flow. The extension of the method, originally
developed for internal flows in highly-curved ducts) 3} to deal with external aerodynamic

The need to derive boundary conditions for the inviscid flow calculations at positions
inside the shear layers that are present in the real viscous flow (RVF) leads naturally to the
concept of an equivalent inviscid flow (EIF*), which can be described (tl'l z) as an analytic
continuation of the external inviscid flow, in an overlapping sense, in the direction normal
to the shear layer. Such a concept--which is an example of the technique known as 'zonal
overlapping'--is completely satisfactory in the framework of an interactive VII scheme, in
which the inviscid flow equations are being solved over the full region with interactive
boundary conditions derived from the current viscous solution. When a converged
solution has been obtained, the associated inviscid component of it forms the (unique)
ElF. As we shall see below, the corresponding 'displacement thickness' is not necessarily
unique (it may depend on the particular definition used or on the position at which the
inviscid boundary condition is being applied), but this does not introduce any ambiguity
into the combined solution. The concept is admittedly more difficult to apply to
experimental measurements of a real flow, where the extrapolation needed to define the
EIF would introduce some uncertainties in the calculation of the integral quantities (see
Ref. 14 for example)--though less than if standard first-order definitions were used.

3.3. THE EFFECT OF NORMAL PRESSURE GRADIENTS

3.4. MATCHING CONDITIONS IN THE WAKE

reasonable it may introduce a certain degree of arbitrariness into the numerical results,
which can be avoided if Melnik's technique is used.

3.6. ASPECTSOF 'DIFFERENTIAL'AND 'INTEGRAL' METHODSFOR TURBULENTBOUNDARY

Much of the analysis in the preceding sections is applicable whether a 'differential' or
'integral' method is used for calculating the development of the boundary layers and wake.
In principle, the differential approach should of course be the more accurate of the two,
but this will only be the case in practice if sufficient care is taken to include the various
'higher-order' effects discussed above. To do this properly requires something approaching
a solution of the full (time-averaged) Navier-Stokes (NS) equations; but the numerical
consequences of the elliptic nature of these equations can sometimes be avoided, as shown
for example by Bradshaw et al. ~3-5~ in their 'higher-order shear-layer' method (mentioned
previously on p. 57). They do this, in the framework of a VII procedure, by carrying out
the basic iterations in two steps. In the first it is assumed that the variation of pressure (in
both streamwise and normal directions) is known from a previous iteration, so that
equations of the boundary-layer type--similar to those used in the 'turbulent energy'
method of Bradshaw et al. ~53~ but with additional higher-order terms included---can be
solved efficiently by marching in the streamwise direction. In the second step, the
momentum equation in the direction normal to the surface is solved to give a better

5. COUPLING METHODS
In the VII method, separate calculations of the outer inviscid flow and the inner viscous
flow are combined iteratively to provide a composite solution to the overall flow. In this
section methods of coupling flows are described and their convergence properties studied.
The VII technique requires the solution of the non-linear equations for the inviscid and
viscous flows with the boundary conditions on the matching surface satisfied
simultaneously by both flows. This simultaneous solution of two non-linear boundaryvalue problems coupled through their boundary conditions is normally accomplished by
an iterative procedure. Thwaites ~76~describes the classical approach for the calculation of
attached flow about an aerofoil. The velocity gradient derived from an inviscid flow
without any representation of viscous effects is used to calculate the development of the
boundary layer. The rate of growth of the boundary layer provides new boundary
conditions for calculating the first inviscid approximation to the viscous flow. This
process is then repeated with the intention of providing better approximations to the
overall flow. It is useful to describe this particular iterative approach as hierarchical since
it assigns a particular order to the calculation; the inviscid flow followed by the viscous
flow.
For a flow with a weak interaction between the viscous and inviscid parts, this classical
iterative procedure provides an adequate estimate of the viscous effects. However, in most
practical flows the interaction is not weak and the iterative scheme rarely converges
without assistance: for flows with separation the iterations are normally divergent. In
Section 3 the extension of the VII technique to flows including separation has been
described so that the challenge is to provide an iterative scheme that provides converged
solutions for flows with strong interaction. There have been two distinct approaches to
defining suitable iterative schemes for flows with separation and they are characterised
by the particular viewpoint taken of the problem of calculating separated flow. The first
view is taken from the perspective of the boundary layer. Catherall and Mangler 177~
demonstrated that the stiffness of the boundary-layer equation at separation is not an
inherent property of these equations but rather a feature of the method of solution. If the
usual direct method of solving the equations, for which the velocity gradient is specified, is
replaced by an inverse method, for which the velocity gradient is calculated, then the
equations can be integrated smoothly through separation. As a consequence the hierarchy
of the direct scheme is inverted to give a fully-inverse method (FI) and closely related semiinverse method (SI). Now the order of calculating the inviscid and viscous flows is inverted
which reflects the increased importance of the viscous layer in separated flow. The second
view focuses on the coupling procedure. As described by Veldman,~78~ neither flow is
dominant, so there is no hierarchy in the flow and a simultaneous solution of both flows is
preferable. This leads to coupling schemes which are simultaneous (S) or quasisimultaneous (QS).
In this section a range of techniques for coupling the flows is described. A unified

coupling should be simultaneous. This feature had already been recognised by Lees and

hyperbolic in nature which allows a numerical solution by a marching procedure. The
boundary-layer equations are also solved by a marching procedure, so the flows can be
solved simultaneously with the Prandtl-Meyer relationship from the inviscid flow applied
as a boundary condition for the solution of the boundary-layer equations. However,
subsonic flow is elliptic in nature and the marching procedure and simple simultaneous
coupling are not possible. The simultaneous coupling scheme has to be extended to
capture the elliptic nature of the external flow.

5.1.5. Quasi-simultaneous Method

The simultaneous solution of the viscous and inviscid flows can be very involved and a

form (81a) or (81b) which are applied as boundary conditions for the inviscid flow. The
next approximation to the matched solution is given by the intersection of the tangent to
the viscous curve with the inviscid curve.

6.1.2. NACA 4412
The stall on the N A C A 4412 aerofoil at a Reynolds number of the order of one million
arises from a trailing-edge separation of the boundary layer which moves towards the
leading edge slowly with increasing incidence. As a result the NACA 4412 is an ideal
section for detailed measurements of a trailing edge separation and measurements have
been made by Wadcock I~°°) and Hastings. t1°1) The ability of the VII method to predict
separated flow is demonstrated by comparison of the semi-inverse method developed by
Williams ~44) with the experimental results of Hastings. ~1°11
In the experiment of Hastings a two-dimensional model with the NACA 4412 section
was tested in the RAE 3.962 m x 2.743 m (13 ft x 9 ft) atmospheric low-speed wind tunnel.
The chord of the model was 1.0 m and it was mounted horizontally spanning the tunnel.
The Reynolds and Mach number of the test were 4.17 x 10 6 and 0.18 respectively. To avoid
the occurrence of laminar separation bubbles, the transition from laminar to turbulent
boundary layer was fixed by placing a single wire at x/c=O.O14 and 0.113 on the upper and
lower surfaces respectively and adding distributed roughness ahead of the transition wires.
Boundary-layer fences were placed along the chord at 0.85 m on either side of the midspan to maintain reasonably steady and uniform separated flow over the mid-portion of
the wing and these were effective up to an angle of incidence of 13 °. The main set of data
was taken at an incidence of 12.15 ° for which the aerofoil has almost attained its
maximum lift coefficient and the separation was at about x/c = 0.8. The lift coefficient was
calculated by integrating the static pressure distribution around the mid-span of the wing.
The calculations are performed in free-air conditions so the experimental results have been
corrected for the effect of tunnel constraint as described in Ref. 3. For the experimental
angle of incidence of 12.15 ° the corrected lift coefficient and incidence are 1.44 and 12.49 °
respectively. A laser Doppler anemometer was used to obtain mean velocity profiles at
eight stations between 0.59 <x/c <0.99, whilst a pitot traverse probe was used at x/c = 0.2
and 0.4.
The theoretical method of Williams is described in Ref. 44, and is a semi-inverse

6.1.3. GA(W)-2 AeroJoil
The GA(W)-2 (13 ~o thick) aerofoil has been used by several authors for calculations
with VII methods and it provides a useful basis for comparison of different techniques.
The GA(W)-2 aerofoil was tested by McGhee et al. ~1°5~ at a Reynolds number of 4.3 x 106.
In the experiment roughness strips were fixed at 7.5 ~o chord on the upper and lower
surfaces in an attempt to fix transition from the laminar to the turbulent boundary layer.
Calculations indicate that these transition trips are not adequate for the complete
incidence range. Beyond a certain angle of incidence a short laminar separation bubble
occurs ahead of the transition trip on the upper surface, whilst on the lower surface the
stagnation point moves downstream past the transition trip as the angle of incidence is
increased. In the calculations transition is determined by a variety of methods which may
have a significant effect on calculating such quantities as drag. A summary of the methods
used by Cebeci et al. ~97) and Melnik and Brook,~34~ with details of transition-fixing, are
given before a comparison of the results is presented.
The method used by Cebeci et al. 07~ is a quasi-simultaneous coupling of an inviscid
flow based on conformal mapping and Fourier analysis (see Halsey~°6)) and a finitedifference solution of the boundary-layer equations. The linearised approximation to the
inviscid flow required by the quasi-simultaneous method is given by a Hilbert integral.
The boundary-layer equations are transformed into a form which is more convenient and
accurate for finite-difference solution, as described by Cebeci and Schimke31 o7~ Transition
from laminar to turbulent boundary layer is determined by Michel's method as described
by Cebeci and Bradshaw31 o8~ If laminar separation occurs before the predicted transition
location then transition is assumed to occur at the start of the laminar separation bubble
rather than more correctly inside the bubble. The turbulence model is base on the eddyviscosity formulation of Cebeci and Smith. ~54~ The linearised approximation to the
inviscid flow is solved, simultaneously with the boundary-layer equations by Keller's ~°9~
box method in a quasi-simultaneous coupling. For the calculation of regions with reversed
flow the 'FLARE' approximation is employed in which the convective term is set equal to
zero in the recirculating region. No second order effects are included in the boundarylayer method (see Section 3.2) nor is the wake curvature effect included in the equivalent
inviscid flow (see Section 3.5). However, some allowance is made for the normal pressure
gradient in the equivalent inviscid flow by satisfying the Kutta condition on the
displacement surface. [A better allowance could be made by satisfying the Kutta condition
at a distance of the momentum thickness plus the displacement thickness from the surface
of the aerofoil ~as described in Section 3.3 and in particular Eq. 39).-I
The semi-inverse method used by Williams is described in the last section and couples a
surface-singularity method for the incompressible inviscid flow with the lag-entrainment
method. It does not contain second order effects in the inviscid or viscous flow, except for
the correction from equivalent inviscid flow to the real viscous flow (see equation 27).
Transition on the upper surface is either fixed at the transition strip or by a semi-empirical
calculation of the laminar separation bubble if laminar separation occurs ahead of the
transition strip. On the lower surface the transition is taken at 2 3 ~ chord for all
incidences.
The GRUMFOIL method developed by Melnik uses a semi-inverse method to couple a
fully conservative compressible potential method to the lag--entrainment method for the
turbulent boundary layer (see Table 1, p. 125). The equivalent inviscid flow contains the

Most transonic aerofoil tests are made in wind tunnels with ventilated (slotted or
perforated) walls; these certainly reduce the overall wall interference but make it
extremely difficult to estimate the residual effects accurately. For this reason it may
sometimes be preferable, at least for free-stream Mach numbers up to about 0.8, to
test in wind tunnels with solid walls, on which the boundary conditions can be
determined precisely.
The spurious effects of the roughness bands normally used to fix transition will be
particularly marked when the local pressure is near-sonic, both as regards the
detailed pressure distribution and the overall forces (particularly drag).
Sidewall support interference is more severe than at low speeds, and when the flow
on the forward part of an airfoil is supersonic disturbances emanating from the
leading-edge/wall junction can propagate in the form of weak shock waves and
spuriously affect the pressures measured at the centre of the wind tunnel.

Mainly for these reasons, we have chosen for illustrative purposes experiments on just
two aerofoils. These are RAE 2822 and RAE 5225.

and the tunnel height/chord ratio 4.0, both satisfactorily high values. Most of the tests

ballotini, usually at 3 % chord on both surfaces; in most cases these were chosen to be of
the minimum size needed to be effective, so that the spurious disturbances they produce
should be small. The main objective of the experiments was to obtain detailed
measurements of the mean velocity profiles in the boundary layers and near wake, and this
makes them particularly suitable as test cases for CFD methods. They were chosen for that
purpose for the 1980-81 Stanford Conference,"2~) and have been used as examples by
many authors.
The 8ft x 6ft wind tunnel has slotted walls (1.6 % open area ratio), and although they

6.4. METHODSFOR THREE-DIMENSIONALFLOWS

Turning finally to the assessment of methods for calculating three-dimensional viscous
flows over wings or wing-fuselage combinations, we shall concentrate here on the wings,
since for bodies the interactive effects are usually small unless separation takes place, in
which case the problem is still currently intractable. Considering that the subject is clearly
of the greatest possible practical importance, it is remarkable how little has been
published about it since the first author's review of 1980.~1~ We consider here only
methods involving a fully three-dimensional treatment of the turbulent boundary layers,
though useful results can sometimes be obtained by using a quasi-two-dimensional 'striptheory' technique. Details have been published about three such methods, which we shall
describe sequentially; since no common test case appears to have been attempted in three
dimensions, we shall list only the main features of the method and then show some
comparisons with experiment.

6.4.1. ONERA (Le Balleur and Lazareff Refs. 10, 18, 67, 128)

based on the family of velocity profiles described in Section 4. This is a 'direct' marching
scheme, with Qiw prescribed, in which the direction of the forward step is allowed to vary
locally in accordance with the characteristic directions.
• Wake: neither the thickness nor curvature effect are included.
• Matching: through the transpiration velocity Wiw with empirical under relaxation:

7. CONCLUSIONS
The general principles of interactive methods for calculating viscous flows are now well
established:
(1) Generalisations of the concept of the displacement effect of the viscous layers, as
modelled through a 'transpiration' velocity through the wall in the equivalent
inviscid flow (EIF), have been derived which enable VII methods to be applied in
many cases for which the usual assumptions of first order boundary-layer theory are
certainly violated.
(2) Similar generalisations of the momentum integral equations allow boundary-layer
methods of 'integral' type to incorporate several 'higher-order' features, in particular
the effects of normal pressure gradients induced by the curvature of the flow and of
Reynolds normal stresses, which would otherwise require a full solution of the
Navier-Stokes (NS) equations to simulate; and this work also suggests that similar
improvements are needed to methods of 'differential' type to bring them up to an
even higher standard.

The importance of properly modelling the near wake region (even though the

itself) is now fully appreciated; and our understanding of how to do this in the
framework of a VII method--involving jumps in the components of velocity both
normal and tangential to the wake surface in the ElF, to allow respectively for the
wake thickness and curvature effects--has been greatly improved.
A similar improvement in our understanding of the mechanism which governs the
coupling between the inviscid and viscous components of a VII method has been
achieved through the numerical stability analysis of Le Balleur and others. In
particular, this work has led (at least in two dimensions) to

In two dimensions: for single aerofoils a number of existing methods give impressive
results even up to high lift coefficients and stalling conditions, except where
appreciable shock-induced separation is involved.
For multiple-element aerofoils at low speeds, good results have also been obtained
for lift but drag predictions are still not satisfactory. The chief obstacle to producing
adequate drag predictions and to progress up to stalling conditions remains the
modelling of the interaction between the wakes from one element and the boundary
layer developing on the next; for this problem local solutions of the NS equations
may be needed.
For wings in three dimensions, the number of published VII methods is relatively
small, and their performance is satisfactory only over a rather narrow range of
'easier' test cases. The most urgent need is for the development of better numerical
coupling techniques for dealing with flows approaching, and beyond, the separation
of the boundary layer; but it is clear also that improvements are needed both to the
inviscid and viscous components of any VII method. For the former, really accurate
solutions of the Euler (rather than the full potential) equations are certainly desirable,
and for the latter, better modelling of the higher-order effects identified as important
in two-dimensional treatment of the subject should be incorporated.
Up to the present, no attempt has been made to model the flow in junction regions-of a wing with a fuselage or pylon with a wing or store--or near the wing tips. Some
progress in this direction is surely possible with interactive methods, even though
eventually no doubt full NS solutions will be needed, at least locally.
Similarly, it is doubtful how far VII techniques can be advanced in the solution of
flow problems involving massive three-dimensional separations, particularly those
involving the formation of discrete vortex sheets. There is some possibility that these
vortices could be at least partially simulated through the inviscid Euler solution, and
their interaction with the boundary layer taken care of by a VII scheme; but it
remains to be seen how effective this might be.

This brings us finally to the main question mark regarding the future of VII methods, as
opposed to full numerical solutions of the NS equations for the complete configuration
under consideration. At present, most NS methods are severely limited by (a) excessively
long computing times as the grid is refined close to the surface and (b) inadequacies in
turbulence modelling. Although recent advances in numerical techniques for solving the
NS equations have been most impressive (see for example Ref. 142), it does seem likely

that similar techniques could also be used to speed up the treatment of the inviscid (Euler)
component of a VII method; so that provided it is also possible to improve the efficiency
of the coupling technique and extend the range of boundary-layer solutions in three
dimensions, the advantages in terms of computing time that the best VII methods
certainly now possess should still be retained. Whether this will continue to outweigh their
undoubted limitations as regards generality of application, is much more doubtful.
