1 INTRODUCTION
Nonlinearities pervade all areas of engineering, and systems
of nonlinear equations arise in connection with numerous
mechanical problems. The forms and properties of these
systems depend strongly on the type of problem and the
inherent nonlinearities. It is well recognized that, except in
special cases, direct methods for solving nonlinear systems
are unavailable or infeasible, and that attention must focus on
iterative processes. The choice and effectiveness of an iterative technique depends critically on the available information
about the particular equation and its solution set, the aims and
accuracy requirements of the computation, and the available

guidelines for deciding on a ‘best’ solution process for any

This chapter gives an overview of some of the basic

3.2

Attractors

As for continuous dynamical systems x′ = G(t, x), t ≥ 0, it is
critically important to gain some insight into the qualitative
properties of the flow (17) of a given iterative process  =
(Ω, N, G).
A set S ⊂ Ω is an invariant set of  if (x) ⊂ S for each
x ∈ S. Invariant sets of special interest are the fixed points of
the system; that is, points x∗ ∈ Ω where (x∗ ) = {x∗ } and
hence the orbit is stationary. In fact, for reasonable G these
are the only points to which any orbit (x) is expected to
converge.
Another example of an invariant set is a periodic orbit,
where xk = x0 for some index k > 0. For example, if the cubic
polynomial x3 − 2x + 2 = 0 is solved by the Newton iteration xk+1 = (2(xk )3 − 2)∕(3(xk )2 − 2), k = 0, 1, 2, … , then
(0) = {0, 1, 0, 1, … }. This 2-cycle is stable in the sense
that for sufficiently small x0 = 𝜀, the points xk of the orbit
with even and odd k tend to 0 and 1, respectively.
An invariant set S ⊂ Ω is an attractor if S is contained
in a set U ⊂ Ω with the properties (i) U is open (in Rn ),
(ii) for each x ∈ U the orbit converges to a point of S, and
(iii) there is no proper, invariant subset of S with the same
property. Thus, a fixed point x∗ is an attractor if it has an open
neighborhood U ⊂ Ω, such that for all x ∈ U the orbit (x)
converges to x∗ . In particular, an open ball B(x∗ , r) centered
at an attractive fixed point x∗ is an attraction ball if the orbit
of each point in the ball converges to x∗ .

3.3

Convergence orders

A comparison of iterative processes requires some measure
of their efficiency and computational cost. The cost of a
process may be quantified by the product of the average cost
of executing one step and the estimated total number of steps
for reaching an acceptable iterate. Both quantities depend on
the specific problem, the choice of the process, and the details
of its implementation.
For a unit of the computational cost, the name horner was
proposed by Ostrowski (1960), but there is no generally
accepted specification of such a unit. Often, a horner is
defined as one call of a scalar function, in which case one
evaluation of a mapping F on Rn , or of its derivative DF(x)
requires n, or n2 horners, respectively.
The number of steps required by an iterative process
 = (Ω, N, G) to reach an acceptable solution depends
strongly on the chosen start point. Accordingly, interest
centers on asymptotic, worst-case measures, which, in
general, involve an assessment of convergence rates. Various
definitions of such rates go back to the beginnings of convergence theory. We summarize here an approach by Ortega
and Rheinboldt (1970), which modeled definitions of rootconvergence factors (R-factors) and quotient-convergence

Basic observations

An orbit of an iterative process  = (Ω, N, G) is uniquely
defined by its start point. But, as we saw, this provides
only limited information about the behavior of the iterates.
As all practical engineering systems are expected to have
effective controls, it is highly desirable to introduce controls
into iterative processes in order to adjust the course of the
computed orbits.
Broadly speaking, control theory concerns the study of
methods for influencing the behavior of dynamical systems.
It has become a large, interdisciplinary field of engineering
and mathematics with major applications and a huge literature. For our discrete-time dynamical systems, it is natural
to consider adaptive controls in order to counteract erratic
behavior of the flow. Adaptive controls have been the subject
of active research for several decades (Sastry and Bodson
(1989), Ioannou and Sun (1996)). For the numerical solution of ordinary differential equations (ODEs), such controls
have been discussed by Gustavson (1991, 1994), Gustavson
and Soederlind (1997), Deuflhard and Bornemann (2002),
Soederlind (2002), and Soederlind and Wang (2005), among
others.
In principle, adaptive controls involve the identification of
suitably chosen system parameters, some of which may be
uncertain. At each state of the process, the controller has to
measure or estimate specific parameters and then use this
information to adapt the state transition so as to meet certain
performance criteria.
The literature on controlling iterative methods for a nonlinear equation (2) is still relatively small. Some basic topics
have been addressed by (Deuflhard, 2004), but, generally,
most known approaches were developed for selected problems and are heuristic in nature.
Let  be an iterative process and assume that the points
x0 , x1 , … , xk ∈ Rn of some orbit have been computed so far.
Then, some typical types of performance measures include
• The residual error ||F(xk )|| and its trend.
• The size of the last step ||xk − xk−1 ||, k ≥ 1.
• The change ||xk − xk−1 ||∕||xk−1 − xk−2 ||, k ≥ 2, of the step
sizes.
• Comparison of computed steps with steps expected from
convergence theorems.
• Estimates of the condition of F at xk and its changes.
• The “total” distance ||xk − x0 || from the start point.
On the basis of these measurements, the aim is to decide
on suitable modifications of the next step of the process.

5.2

Inexact Newton methods

At each step of a linearization method (37), the linear system
Bk (x − xk ) = F(xk ) has to be solved. For large dimensions,
this may require the use of a secondary linear iterative
process such as SOR (successive over relaxation), ADI (alternating direction implicit), or a Krylov method. We consider
here only the representative example of Newton’s method as
the primary process, in which case the linear system has the
form DF(xk )y = F(xk ). A secondary linear iteration provides
only an approximate solution ŷ k and, most likely, at the next
iterate xk+1 = xk + ŷ k the residual
rk = DF(xk )(xk+1 − xk ) + F(xk )

(42)

is not zero. The norm ||F(xk )|| represents a measure of the
residual of the primary process. As long as this primary
residual is still relatively large, there is little reason for
enforcing a very small ||rk ||. Hence, the secondary process
should be terminated adaptively, based, for example, on
the quotient ||rk ||∕||F(xk )|| of the secondary and primary
residuals. Combined processes with Newton’s method as
primary iteration and an adaptive control for the secondary
method usually carry the name inexact Newton methods,
introduced by Dembo et al. (1982).

6.1

Introduction

Most practical problems incorporate various parameters and
hence involve equations of the form
F(y, 𝜆) = 0, F ∶E⊂Rn ∶= Rm × Rd → Rm , n = m + d, d >0
(50)
with a state variable y ∈ Rm and a parameter vector 𝜆 ∈ Rd .
Some parameters may characterize problem features that are
assigned specific values at the outset; but others are variables,
for which the values depend on the states of the system. We
disregard here all parameters with a-priori-fixed values and
hence assume that the d-dimensional parameter vector 𝜆 is a
system variable. Then the solutions of the underdetermined
equations (50) form a subset M of Rn . Often, M has the
structure of a differentiable submanifold of Rn , and in many
mechanical applications this is called the equilibrium surface
of the problem.
It rarely makes sense to determine only a few specific solutions of a given equation (50); instead, interest centers on
analyzing the form of the solution set in certain regions of
the parameters. In particular, it is important to detect parameter configurations, where the solution behavior exhibits
major changes, as, for instance, where a mechanical structure
buckles or collapses. Thus, typically, applications involve
computational problems of the following type:
• Calculation of specific curves on the solution set M.
• Calculation of a triangulation (or mesh) on parts of M.
• Determination of properties of M, for example, of the
curvature at specific points.
• Computation of singularities on M.
• Identification of the solution behavior near singular
points.
• Determination of stability boundaries of a structure.

In essence, bifurcation theory concerns the study of sudden
changes in the qualitative structure of a mathematical system.
A major part of the theory concerns parameterized dynamical
systems, where a local bifurcation is said to occur when a
small change of some parameter values causes sudden topological changes of the local flow structure and/or modifications of the stability properties of certain invariant sets.
The name bifurcation was introduced in 1882 by Henri
Poincaré in his study of certain problems of celestial
mechanics. Since then the concept has evolved considerably. In some classical sense, it refers to changes in the
multiplicity of solutions, and, more generally, topological
approaches provide a relation with the theory of singularities
of mappings; see, for example, Golubitsky and Schaeffer
(1985) and Golubitsky et al. (1988) for a detailed analysis.
A related, major topic is the stability of the systems under
consideration. A historical overview of the structural stability
of mechanical structures is given by Bazant (2000). In 1744
Leonard Euler first determined the critical load of an elastic
column when the equilibrium state becomes instable. In 1788
Joseph-Louis Lagrange defined the stability of equilibria
of conservative systems in terms of the potential energy.
Thereafter, consideration of dynamic instabilities led to more
general stability definitions, including various approaches
involving responses to small changes of system-data, and
the stability theory of flows by A. M. Ljapunov in 1893. By
now the literature on bifurcation theory, its applications, and
computational aspects, has become very extensive; see, for
example, the books Chow and Hale (1982), Hale and Kocak
(1991), Kuznewtsov (1995), and Govaerts (2000).
In accordance with the topic of this chapter, consideration
is restricted to some bifurcation problems arising in connection with nonlinear equations. In that case, bifurcation theory
concerns mainly changes of the multiplicity of solutions.
There are various different definitions of such changes; we
use here the following one introduced by Marsden (1978):

7.1

Algorithmic approaches

A natural approach for the study of the form and properties of
a submanifold M of Rn is the computation of a triangulation
of M.
Recall that d + 1 points x0 , x1 , … , xd ∈ Rn are called independent if xj − x0 ∈ Rn , j = 1, … , d, are linearly independent vectors. Then the convex hull 𝜎 d ∶= co(x0 , … , xd ) is a
d-dimensional simplex in Rn , (or d-simplex, for short), and
the points are called its vertices. Each nonempty subset of
of the vertices also forms a simplex, called a face of 𝜎 d . A
simplicial complex  in Rn is a finite collection of simplices,
such that each face of a simplex of  also belongs to , and
the intersection of two simplices of  is either empty or a
simplex of .
A triangulation of a subset S ⊂ Rn is a simplicial complex
 for which
• the vertices of all simplices are points of S,
• the simplices of  have dimension at most d ≤ n, and
• each k-simplex, k < d, in  is a face of at least one
d-simplex of the complex.
There is a large literature on various types of triangulations.
In particular, triangulations play a fundamental role in the
solution of space-discretized partial differential equations,
for example, by means of the finite element method. They are
also required in computer-aided geometric design (CAGD)
and related applications.
In many of these particular problems, the computational
tasks differ considerably from those here under consideration. For example, in most cases of a boundary value problem
for a partial differential equation, the set to be triangulated
is specified explicitly. In CAGD problems, certain submanifolds of R2 or R3 are considered, but, typically, they are
defined as the image sets of prescribed parameterizations,

and hence the corresponding triangulation methods are fairly
similar to those used in the triangulation of domains of
boundary value problems.
For a submanifold M ⊂ Rn that is defined as the solution set
of a nonlinear equation, there are as yet not many methods.
Piecewise linear methods were used by Allgower and
Georg (2003) to construct an n-dimensional simplicial
complex in the ambient space Rn . Then the barycenters of
appropriate faces of the enclosing simplices were chosen
to define a piecewise linear approximation of M. However,
in general, the resulting vertices do not lie on the manifold, and the approach is limited to problems in relatively
low-dimensional ambient spaces.
For an implicitly defined two-dimensional submanifold M
of R3 , Melville and Mackey suggested a method where M
is tessellated by a complex of nonoverlapping cells with
piecewise curved boundaries. These cells are constructed by
tracing fish-scale patterns of one-dimensional paths on the
manifold. The method appears to be intrinsically restricted to
two-dimensional manifolds. Later, Henderson (2002) developed a related approach, where a d-dimensional manifold,
d ≥ 2, is covered by a set of overlapping d-dimensional balls
and the boundary of the union of the balls is expressed in
terms of a set of finite, convex polyhedra.

ONE-DIMENSIONAL SUBMANIFOLDS

8.1

Continuation methods

Let M denote a one-dimensional C1 -submanifold M of Rn .
Then, a connected component of M is either diffeomorphic
to the unit circle in the plane, or to an interval on the real
line. Methods for approximating such connected components
have been developed in various applications under different
names, such as, “continuation methods”, “imbedding methods”, “homotopy methods”, “parameter variation methods”,
or “incremental methods”. Over time, the approaches have
begun to merge and the names haves lost some of their
specificity.
Basically, a continuation method is a method that starts
with a known point x0 on M and generates successively a
sequence of further points x1 , x2 , x3 , … on the manifold.
Typically, at xk the method constructs a local parameterization Φ of M and then computes xk+1 = Φ(s) with a suitably chosen value s in the domain of Φ. As discussed
before, an evaluation of a local parameterization requires an
iterative process for the solution of a nonlinear system of
equations. Adaptive controls have to be used for the construction of the various quantities. In other words, a continuation
method represents a discrete-time dynamical process, which
provides for the computation of the orbit {xk } of a given start
point x0 .
The literature on the design, and application of continuation methods is huge and cannot be covered here. A

FURTHER TOPICS

In some applications, it turns out that no particular intrinsic
parameter is very practical for computational use. Here, the
so-called homotopy methods can come into play, where an
artificial parameter is introduced as a computational tool.
Two continuous mappings Fi ∶ E ⊂ Rn → Rm , i = 1, 2, are
homotopic if there exists a continuous mapping H ∶ EH ⊂
Rn × R1 → Rm , E × [0, 1] ⊂ EH , such that H(x, 0) = F1 (x)
and H(x, 1) = F2 (x) for x ∈ E. A principal question of homotopy theory concerns the preservation of the solvability properties of the equations H(x, 𝜆) = 0 when 𝜆 moves from 0 to 1.
Such information can be a powerful theoretical and computational tool.
Since, in practice, iterative processes can be guaranteed
to converge to a zero x2 ∈ Rn of F2 only if started sufficiently near the point, there is much interest in techniques
which “globalize” the process. For this, let H be a homotopy that “connects” F2 to a mapping F1 for which a zero
x1 ∈ Rn is already known. If the homotopy preserves the
solvability properties, then we expect the solutions x = x(𝜆)
of the intermediate systems H(x, 𝜆) = 0 to form a path from
x1 to x2 . The idea of the homotopy methods is to reach x2 by
following this path computationally, for example, by means
of some of the methods discussed earlier.
Homotopies arise naturally in connection with simplicial
approximations of mappings. A result of algebraic topology
states that a continuous mapping F between certain subsets
of finite-dimensional spaces can be approximated arbitrarily

algorithms utilizing simplicial approximations were given
in 1964 by Lemke and Howson and addressed the numerical solution of linear complementarity problems. In 1967,
Scarf introduced algorithms for approximating fixed points
of continuous mappings, which were later shown to belong to
the same class of methods. Since then the literature on piecewise linear methods has grown rapidly.
Piecewise linear methods have found applications in
various areas, as, for example, in economic equilibrium
studies, and certain integer programming problems. They
have also been used for the computation of stationary points
of optimization problems on polytopes, and the approximation of paths in linear spaces. The methods also provide
a computationally implementable proof of the Brouwer
fixed-point theorem. A survey of the area with extensive
references was given by Allgower and Georg (2000).
The piecewise linear methods do not require smooth
mappings and hence have a theoretically broad range of
applicability. In fact, they have also been extended to the
computation of fixed points of set-valued mappings. But
usually the methods are considered to be less competitive
whenever more detailed information about the structure and
smoothness of F permits the utilization of other techniques.
This appears to be one of the reasons why these methods
have not found extensive use in computational mechanics.
Symmetry is a natural phenomenon in many applications and often reflects some invariance properties
of the problem. For example, the unfolded pitchfork
problem F(y, 𝜆, 𝜇) ∶= 𝜆y − y3 − 𝜇 is covariant under the
Z2 -symmetry F(−y, 𝜆, −𝜇) = −F(y, 𝜆, 𝜇). There is a large
literature on symmetry behavior and, in particular, on
symmetry breaking at bifurcation points of F. Group
theoretical methods can be used effectively in numerical
computations, for example, for detecting and evaluating
bifurcation points. Symmetries also allow for the system to
be restricted to certain subspaces defined by the groups under
consideration. In addition, symmetries help in designing
orderings of the computed results. The computational
aspects of this topic were surveyed, for example, by Dellnitz
and Werner (1989).
Many problems in engineering tend to arise in the
form of boundary value problems for partial differential
equations. In other words, the resulting parameterized
equations F(y, 𝜆) = 0 involve an operator F ∶ X × Rd → Y
between infinite-dimensional spaces X, Y, for example,

25

Banach spaces. For the computation, the system has to be
discretized; that is, a family Fh ∶ X × Rd → Y of discrete
approximations of F has to be constructed. This raises the
question about approximation theorems, which guarantee
the convergence of Fh to F as the discretization parameter
h ∈ R1 tends to zero and provide estimates of the approximation errors. Such results open up possibilities of comparing
the solution sets of F and Fh and their structural properties,
and of determining if singularities of F are approximated
by singularities of Fh of corresponding type. Results along
this line were surveyed, for example, by Caloz and Rappaz
(1997).
