INTRODUCTION

To carry out a finite element analysis (FEA or FEM), or
any type of analyses such as a boundary element method
(BEM) or a finite volume method (FVM) which requires
using a spatial decomposition of the domain of interest, it
is necessary to construct an appropriate mesh of the corresponding computational domain. This is the first step we
face when using such methods. There are various automatic mesh-generation methods that are widely used in
software packages (Frey and George, 2000). Nevertheless,
these methods are subject to rapid changes and improvements and the demand in terms of meshing facilities is
also constantly changing. At present, developing quality
meshes adapted to the physics of the problem to be solved
represents a field of intensive researches (Thompson et al.,
1999).
Meshes can be categorized as being structured or unstructured. Structured meshes follow a simple structure like a
grid or a finite difference network at the vertex connectivity level. Construction methods for such meshes are purely
algebraic (Cook, 1974), of the multiblock type (Allwright,
1988), or based on appropriate PDE solutions (Thompson
et al., 1985). Geometries that can be successfully handled

Mesh Generation and Mesh Adaptivity: Theory and Techniques

are more or less close to quadrilateral (hexahedral) regions
or decomposed by means of such simply shaped regions.
Arbitrarily shaped domains cannot be handled easily with
such methods and, hence, unstructured meshes appear to
be much more versatile. Various ways of considering this
mesh-generation problem have been devised in a number
of manuscripts (Knupp and Steinberg, 1993; George, 1996;
Löhner, 1997). On the one hand, the mesh of the domain is
obtained through a twofold procedure involving the construction of a boundary mesh followed by the generation of
the mesh of the spatial domain defined by this boundary
mesh. On the other hand, global methods allow one to
consider both stages at the same time. We have deliberately chosen to follow a different methodology based on a
more historical perspective, where the methods are described
according to their technical aspects (data structures and
algorithms).
Considered from this standpoint, construction methods
fall into three main categories: hierarchical spatial decompositions (quadtree, octree) (Yerry and Shephard, 1984;
Schneiders, 2000), advancing-front strategies (Van Phai,
1982; Lo, 1985; Löhner, 1997), and Delaunay-type methods,
(Weatherill and Hassan, 1994; Marcum and Weatherill,
1995; George and Borouchaki, 1998). Before going into
further detail, we should mention that constructing a mesh
in any case involves (i) creating vertices and (ii) connecting
these vertices to build mesh elements. If, for any reason, one
of these two stages fails, no mesh will be produced at all.
Therefore, the main issue is to design and implement robust
algorithms.
So far, the discussion has concerned the so-called classical mesh-generation problem in which, given a boundary
discretization, the goal is to achieve, at best, a mesh of
the domain made up of elements that may be considered
as reasonable in terms of both their size and their quality
shape. The size is related to the greater or lesser thickness of the boundary discretization and remains to be properly defined inside the domain. The quality is related to
the element aspect ratios. Moreover, the way in which
these two parameters vary in a given region is an important issue. It could be observed that these criteria greatly
depend on the way in which the field points are created, such
an issue being somewhat cumbersome. Mesh adaptation
involves looking at the same problem while adding a series
of constraints about element size and element directions
(anisotropic case) (Peraire et al., 1987, 1992; D’Azevedo and
Simpson, 1991; Vallet, 1992). The aim is no longer to obtain
the best possible mesh (in an intuitive sense) but to complete
good quality elements of a prescribed size and directions
where necessary. Tree-based methods are unlikely to be suitable in this respect (in particular for handling nonuniform
directional constraints). Advancing-front or Delaunay-type

methods promise greater flexibility and so are more likely
to be suitable.
The chapter is made up of 16 sections. Section 1
provides a general introduction to the various problems
in hand. Section 2 is a brief history about mesh-generation
methods. Section 3 gives an overview of the most popular
mesh-generation methods with a special emphasis on automated methods. Section 4 is the core of the chapter and
contains a number of issues related to adaptation methods.
More precisely, we introduce the notion of a regular mesh.
We then demonstrate how this framework allows a formulation of the mesh-generation problem for adapted meshes.
Then, in the following sections, we discuss the construction
methods based on these ideas. Section 5 deals with curve
discretization. In Section 6, we consider simplicial meshes
and we focus on Delaunay-type methods for surface, planar
and volume domains while giving some comments about the
other generic methods. Section 7 considers, in a similar way,
non-simplicial meshes. Optimization methods are discussed
in Section 8, while Section 9 considers how to mesh a
domain using remeshing techniques. High-order meshes
are described in Section 10. Section 11 establishes the
relationship between adapted mesh-generation methods and
adaptive numerical simulations for a variety of problems.
Moving boundary issues are addressed in Section 12. After
which, large-size mesh creation is discussed, leading to
the topic of parallel meshing, Section 13. To demonstrate
how the previous approaches work, Section 14 shows a
series of application examples in different engineering disciplines together with other more or less derived applications.
Section 15 discusses a number of important implementation
issues mentioning basic, subtle, or even surprising things
that occur when translating a given method in terms of algorithms and computer procedures. Finally, Section 16 gives
some conclusions and looks at what the future may hold.

A BRIEF HISTORY

Without fear of contradiction, it can be claimed that the
FEM was pioneered by engineers and practitioners in the
early fifties; see earlier editions of (Zienkiewicz, 1977),
for instance. Then, during the sixties, mathematicians
established the theoretical and mathematical foundations
of this method; see Ciarlet (1991), Raviart and Thomas
(1998) or Hughes (1998) among many other references.
The FEM then became widely used by various categories of people. A number of applications in different
fields of engineering motivated the development of
mesh-generation methods. Except when considering a
unit square region or simply shaped geometries where
mesh generation is straightforward, concrete applications

As for three dimensions, computer facilities available

three-dimensional meshes. Advancing-front, octree-based,

Carey, 1997; George and Borouchaki, 1998; Frey and
George, 2000, 2001; Löhner, 2008), a handbook (Thompson
et al., 1999), and the present encyclopedia, together with
chapters presented at “specialized” annual conferences and
various survey chapters.

3 CLASSICAL MESH-GENERATION
METHODS
Specific geometries can be handled by means of specific
methods. Domains of a peculiar shape can be dealt with
by taking advantage of their specificities. Convex domains
(with no hole) more or less close to a (deformed) quadrilateral (hexahedron) can be dealt with using algebraic methods
(Cook, 1974) or a partial differential equation (PDE)-based
method, (Thompson et al., 1985). Arbitrary domains can be
decomposed using a number of such simply shaped regions
and then, multiblock methods allow for a solution (Allwright,
1988). Nevertheless, splitting a domain in this way is a
nonobvious task and thus one that calls for fully automated
methods. These methods fall into three categories. The first
type makes use of a hierarchical spatial decomposition that
results in constructing a tree, which, in turn, allows the mesh
elements to be created. The second type of method can be
seen as a greedy algorithm where a piece of the domain
that has not yet been meshed is covered by a mesh element,

3

thus resulting in a “smaller” void region to be considered.
Methods of the third type involve revisiting Delaunay triangulation algorithms so as to produce meshes (and not only
triangulation). The following gives a brief description of
these three types of methods.
Planar and volume domains Before entering into this
description, we give an indication of what we term a
mesh-generation problem.1 We are given a domain Ω in ℝ2
or ℝ3 . This domain is defined by its boundary, Γ, which,
in turn, is known by means of a discretization. The latter
is a collection of line segments in two dimensions, these
segments defining a polygonal approximation of Γ. In three
dimensions, the boundary is defined by means of a triangulated surface (a list of triangles or quadrilaterals) which,
again, defines an approximation of Γ. The problem is then,
using this sole data, to construct an appropriate mesh of Ω
(actually, an approximation of Ω). Such a context defines
a so-called classical mesh-generation problem where the
goal is to recover Ω by quality elements where the notion
of quality only refers to aspect ratio (or element shape) and
mesh gradation but does not explicitly include any information about element sizing. Specifying sizing or directional
requirements leads to a so-called adapted or controlled
mesh-generation problem where the targeted elements, as
above, must be well shaped, nicely graded and, in addition,
must conform to a given sizing or directional specification
(referred to as a metric in the following). The description
below mainly concerns a classical mesh-generation problem,
while a large part of the rest of the chapter discusses other
mesh-generation issues (i.e., how to complete an adapted
mesh and therefore access adaptive computations).
Surface domains Surface domains are different from planar
or volume domains in the sense that a surface mesh must
conform to the geometry of the surface in hand. In other
words, smoothness, quality, and gradation are demanded, but
it is mandatory to match the geometry. This leads to paying
particular attention to the surface curvatures, thus making a
purely two-dimensional approach unlikely to be suitable. In
short, there are two ways to define a surface. A parametric
definition involves defining a parametric space (therefore,
in two dimensions) together with an appropriate mapping
function. A discrete definition allows the geometry to be
described by means of a mesh that is, a priori, a geometric
mesh and not a computational mesh. To some extent, parametric surfaces can be meshed using a two-dimensional
method, provided information about the geometry is used to
govern the method. On the other hand, discrete surfaces must
be considered using the geometric properties of the surface
directly.

Quadtree/octree-based methods

Quadtree (octree)-based mesh-generation methods are
methods whereby the domain is covered by elements
using a recursive subdivision scheme based on a spatial
tree structure (Yerry and Shephard, 1983; Schneiders and
Bünten, 1995; Greaves and Borthwic, 1999; Schneiders,
2000; Fischer and Bar-Yosep, 2000; Marechal, 2001).
A given discretization (a surface mesh) of the boundary
of the domain is first enclosed in a square (a cube). This
initial box (the root of the tree structure) is then recursively
subdivided into four (eight) similar sub-boxes, until a certain
consistency is attained between the boxes and the boundary
items. This recursive scheme can also be envisaged as a tree
decomposition procedure. Once the decomposition has been
achieved, mesh elements are generated by subdividing tree
cells in a conforming way (using predefined patterns). An
optimization stage is usually required as a final step as the
intersections between tree cells and the boundary items may
lead to the creation of ugly-shaped elements.
Tree-based methods are very versatile and are
able to handle complex geometries. However, unlike
advancing-front or Delaunay-based methods, the original
boundary discretization is usually not preserved in the final
mesh of the domain. A variant of this approach consists
in considering that the domain boundary is known via a
geometric modeling system, a computer aided design (CAD)
modeler (no input boundary discretization is supplied). The
insertion of boundary items in the tree structure is then
performed through geometric queries using the CAD
definition.
Tree construction The general scheme for constructing a
spatial decomposition of a computational domain was originally proposed by (Yerry and Shephard, 1983). It consists in
two successive steps: (i) constructing a spatial covering up
from a bounding box of the domain and (ii) creating internal
vertices and mesh elements. It can be seen as an incremental
method that results in inserting a boundary item into a current
spatial decomposition.
Assuming that the domain boundaries are known via a
discretization, an initial cell is created corresponding to the
bounding box of the domain. All boundary items are iteratively inserted into the cells of the current decomposition with respect to their dimension (from points to faces).
Given the tree structure at some iteration, the next entity
E is inserted as follows. The cell C containing E is identified (here, the tree structure proves useful for searching
purposes). If C is empty (no other entity has been associated with it), then E is associated with C. If not, cell
C is subdivided into equally sized sub-cells (four in two
dimensions and eight in three dimensions) and the process

is repeated in these sub-cells. At completion, a tree structure is defined, in which the depth (the maximum level of
refinement) corresponds to the minimal distance between
boundary items. In order to simplify the creation of elements
and to improve the gradation from element to element whatever the targeted elements, this tree structure is balanced so
as to reduce the size ratio between adjacent cells to a factor
of two at most (the 21 ∶ 1 rule).
This method always provides a valid decomposition (there
is no problem of existence) and thus leads to a valid mesh.
Nevertheless, the resulting mesh may not be topologically equivalent to the domain or an accurate geometric
approximation.
Variants of this classical algorithm have been proposed
to relate the local box sizes to the surface curvatures, or to
any prescribed size map (Marechal, 2001; Baehmann et al.,
1987).
Mesh element construction The quadtree/octree method is
not limited to generating triangles or tetrahedra, as the
Delaunay and advancing-front methods are, but can also
produce pure and conformal quadrilateral or hexahedral
meshes.
The creation of internal mesh vertices and mesh elements
is more or less straightforward in the case where simplicial elements are demanded. All internal cell corners are
considered as mesh vertices. In short, the cells of the tree
are subdivided into simplices by means of a reduced number
of templates. First, we make use of the tree-balancing rule
(namely, the 2 ∶ 1 rule) to obtain a substantial reduction in
the number of possible configurations for a given cell, as
compared with its neighbors. Then, a predefined pattern is
associated with each internal cell configuration, in order to
provide a conforming mesh of the cell (16 templates exist in
two dimensions and 78 in three dimensions). The boundary
cells (intersected by the boundary discretization) can be
treated in a similar way. The mesh of the bounding box of the
domain is then obtained by merging all the elements created
at the cell level. To obtain the final mesh of the domain,
a coloring procedure is used to remove external elements.
Obviously, a mesh optimization procedure must be applied
to get rid of the badly shaped elements that may have been
created because of cell/surface intersections and to improve
the overall mesh quality.
The same operation is slightly different in the case where
quads/hexes are demanded. To complete a conformal mesh,
there is no way to subdivide the cells of the tree using
templates as above. Therefore, it is necessary to consider
the dual of these cells. Once the dual cells are defined, we
have a (very) reduced number of templates that allow the
mesh elements to be generated (typically only two in two
dimensions and four in three dimensions), see Figure 1. The

octree decomposition consists in building a surface mesh
from an analytical surface representation, usually known via
a geometric modeler. The depth of the tree can indeed be
related to edge length h and to the size b of the bounding
box by relation p = log2 (b∕h). As edge size h is proportional to the minimum of the principal radii of curvature,
denoted 𝜌, a lower bound for the tree depth can be found:
p ≥ log2 (b∕𝛼 𝜌), where 𝛼 is related to the geometric approximation. The local intrinsic properties of the surface (principal radii of curvature, vertex normals, etc.) can be used to
construct the tree. In practice, the cells are refined until the
geometric approximation criterion is satisfied.

3.2

Advancing-front methods

Advancing-front methods take the given boundary
discretization (mesh) and create the mesh elements within
the domain, advancing in from the boundary until the entire
domain has been covered with elements. Such an element
is constructed on the basis of one entity (edge or triangle)
of a so-called front (initiated by the boundary items) that
is connected with a node appropriately chosen among the
existing nodes or created according to some quality criteria,
Figure 2. Once an element has been formed, the front is
updated and the process is repeated until the current front
is empty (George, 1971; Seveno, 1997; Löhner and Parikh,
1988; Kallinderis et al., 1995; Löhner ,1996).
Advancing-front methods were primarily developed to
handle planar and volume domains but can be used to
construct surface meshes.
Point creation, point connection Formally speaking, the
advancing-front procedure is an iterative procedure, which
attempts to fill the as-yet unmeshed region of the domain
with elements (George, 1971). At each step, a front entity
is selected and a new (optimal) vertex is created and inserted
in the current mesh if it forms a new well-shaped element.
The boundary discretization should be orientable. The mesh
elements are created on the basis of entities (edges or faces)
of the current front (the initial front being the boundary
discretization). Central to the advancing-front technique, the
creation and insertion of an optimal point from a selected
front entity requires some care. At first, an optimal point is

Surface meshing versus advancing front methods The same
concept can be applied to create surface meshes. The main
difference lies in the iterative algorithm used to find an
optimal point location given a front edge. Given an edge AB,
the optimal point P is computed so as to construct an optimal
triangle on the surface. The third point P is determined using
an angle 𝛼 between the stretching direction and the tangent

at the midpoint M of AB (or using an average tangent plane
and setting 𝛼 to zero). This point does not necessarily belong
to the surface, as it has been created in a local tangent plane.
If the surface is known via a geometric modeler, a query can
provide the closest point from P onto the surface. Candidate
points are identified as those lying in the disk of center P and
radius 𝜅 × 𝛿, where 𝜅 is a positive coefficient and 𝛿 denotes
the radius of a region near P. The size of the triangle is locally
adapted to the surface curvature, that is, it is proportional to
the minimal radius of curvature.

3.3

Delaunay-type methods

Many people find Delaunay-type methods very appealing,
as the keyword Delaunay has a touch of elegance based on
various theoretical issues; see Preparata and Shamos (1985)
and Boissonnat and Yvinec (1997) about such triangulations.
Despite this, a more subtle analysis and actual experience
indicate that such theoretical issues must be revisited to be
usable in the context of finite element (FE) meshes. Therefore, Delaunay triangulation algorithms must be usefully
modified so as to be included in the so-called Delaunay
mesh-generation methods.
Delaunay triangulation While alternative methods exist,
a popular and straightforward method for generating a
Delaunay triangulation of a given set of points in ℝ2 or
ℝ3 is the so-called Bowyer–Watson algorithm (Watson,
1981), also developed by Hermeline around the same time
(Hermeline, 1980). It is an incremental method that results
in inserting one point in a given Delaunay triangulation.

Delaunay-based meshing algorithms The above constrained incremental method can be used as part of a meshing
algorithm (thus referred to as Delaunay-based). Let Ω be
the domain to be meshed and let Γ be a discretization of its
boundary. The set of points in Γ is triangulated using the

7

above incremental method (after being included in a convex
bounding box). The resulting triangulation is a triangulation
of the initial box where, in general, extracting a mesh of
Ω is unlikely to be possible. This is due to the fact that the
boundary entities are not necessarily edges or facets of this
triangulation. In other words, inserting the two endpoints of
a boundary edge (the three vertices of a boundary triangle)
may result in a triangulation where this edge (triangle) does
not exist.
Various methods have been developed to allow for the
regeneration of such missing entities. In two dimensions,
edge swapping (flip) operators, Section 8, result in what is
needed, while in three dimensions the same operators are
notably insufficient to achieve the same goal. Indeed, we
face here a problem of existence as with the advancing-front
approach for which a theoretical issue can lead to a practical,
although rather technical, computer implementation (George
et al., 1991b; Weatherill and Hassan, 1994; George and
Borouchaki, 1998, 2002; Si and Gärtner, 2011). Actually,
such methods readily work and result in a triangulation of
the box where all the boundary entities are established. Then
a mesh of Ω can be obtained by suppressing those elements
in the box outside the domain.
For the moment, we have in hand a mesh of Ω whose
vertices are, roughly speaking, the boundary vertices. Such a
mesh is not suitable for FE computations. Therefore, field
points must be created before being inserted. Methods to
create the necessary field points include using the centroid
or the circumcenter of some mesh elements based on appropriate criteria (such as density requirement, element quality

In a classical context, two types of boundary discretizations
can be envisaged. The first case concerns uniform discretizations where a constant stepsize is given. The main advantage
of such a discretization is that, in principle, it is possible to
complete a regular mesh. Nevertheless, this does not guarantee a good approximation of the domain boundaries for a
given step size. Given a uniform discretization of a domain
boundary, a regular mesh is nothing more than a mesh where
the element sizes are “equal” to the step size serving at
the boundary discretization. Thus, the desired size for the
elements in the mesh is known a priori at each mesh vertex in
the regular domain mesh. Let us consider the case where the
domain boundary is composed of several connected components and where these are discretized by means of different
step sizes (as is the case for the domains encountered in
computational fluid dynamics (CFDs)). A regular mesh of
such a domain is a mesh where the element sizes in the
neighborhood of each component are close to the step size
of this component. As for the element sizes elsewhere in the
domain, they must be close to the step sizes of the discretization of the boundaries situated in some neighborhood.
The second type of discretization concerns the so-called
geometric discretizations that are adapted to the boundary
geometries. In this case, it may be proved that the discretization step size must be locally proportional to the minimum
radius of curvature of the boundary. The drawback of this
type of discretization is that rather large variations in the
discretization step size may result. In order to avoid this
phenomenon, a smoothing technique on the discretization
step size may be applied. For a geometric discretization (not
uniform in general) of the domain boundaries, it is difficult to
determine a priori what element sizes make the mesh regular.
Obviously, a regular mesh is one where the element sizes are
almost constant (or vary just a little). The idea is then to find
among all the continuous size functions that which leads to
a minimal variation. This notion of a minimal variation is
a characterization, among others, of the surfaces defined by
means of harmonic functions.

3.4

Combined methods

The three main classes of automated mesh-generation
methods have their own advantages and drawbacks.
Combining one or more of these methods is therefore
an elegant way to benefit from the advantages of such or
such a method while avoiding any possible drawbacks. In
this respect, Delaunay-type methods have been combined
with octree or advancing-front techniques and, conversely,
octree or advancing-front techniques have been combined
with Delaunay methods (Mavriplis, 1990; Mavriplis, 1995;
Rebay, 1993; Marcum and Weatherill, 1995), and so on.

4.3

Mesh gradation

According to a metric specification, mesh gradation can
obviously be controlled by changing the mesh size specification rather than modifying the mesh generator itself. This
can be achieved by applying a metric smoothing method
that consists in replacing a metric by an average of the
neighboring metrics (Borouchaki et al., 1997). Notice that
this method does not explicitly control the mesh gradation.
Moreover, if the latter is not satisfactory, the smoothing
procedure must be iterated.
It is clear that the mesh gradation can be improved if
we reduce the mesh size in any direction, and possibly by
preserving the metric shape (the metric is locally proportional to the initial one). Hence, the mesh gradation is
controlled by analyzing all mesh edges, replacing, if necessary, the size specifications at the endpoints and iterate if
a modification occurs. In the isotropic case where the size
specification is defined as a function h, the mesh gradation
purpose is to bound the norm of the gradient of function h or
the ratio of the Euclidean length for two adjacent edges in the
resulting mesh. In the anisotropic case, the same ideas apply.

MESH OPTIMIZATION

Mesh optimization has proved useful in three different
contexts: it could be part of a mesh generation method (in
which it is the final step of the overall procedure); it could
be seen as a stand-alone process or, finally, it can be used for
remeshing purposes (see the next section).
Optimizing a mesh can be achieved by means of two
different strategies. A global view relies on solving a
PDE-based optimization problem where the cost function
is related to one or several mesh quality criteria (Freitag
and Plassmann, 2000; Knupp, 2001). Cost considerations
together with the fact that optimality is not really needed
pushed many authors toward using local heuristics.
So far, the proposed mesh generation methods result in a
unit mesh of domain Ω. Nevertheless, the mesh quality can be
improved by means of several optimization processes, some
made up of topological modifications and others consisting
of geometrical modifications. The first, suitable for simplicial
meshes, mainly involves applying a number of facet flips
while the other consists of node repositioning and node

midpoint of the edge. Conversely, the edge removal operation (edge collapse) consists in contracting the edge to its
midpoint or one of its extremities (if it is an internal edge
with an extremity belonging to the boundary).

A loop of (adaptive) simulations contains a number of iterations, each of which includes a meshing step. The mesh at a

given step can be constructed by means of a mesh generation
method governed in some way or, alternatively, by means
of a remeshing method. The current mesh is modified and
updated so as to fulfill some (metric) requirements. Both
approaches have proved to be of interest. The first approach
has been already seen, and in this section we discuss the
remeshing strategy.

9.1

Unit discrete curve rediscretization

A discrete curve is an assembly of line segments (defined
by an ordered set of sampling points). These points can,
for instance, be generated by a CAD system or by a scanning device, or they can be the result of a numerical simulation in an adaptive scheme. Prior to rediscretizing the
curve, we must define a smooth geometrical support (interpolating curve) associated with the discrete curve. Indeed,
this support will be used to position the vertices of the
envisioned discretization. In addition, if the data is “noisy”
because of measurements or computation errors, resulting
in an interpolating curve with a rough aspect, smoothing
techniques, including various averaging schemes, can be
applied.
Formally, given a set of points {Pi }i=0, … ,n , constructing
the support requires finding a continuous function 𝛾 defined
on ℝ with values in ℝd (in the two- or three-dimensional
space), and increasing real numbers ti such that 𝛾(ti ) = Pi
for each i = 0, … , n. In practice, the solution can be chosen
among piecewise polynomial functions called splines, by
analogy to the draftsman’s tool consisting of a thin flexible
rod made of metal, plastic, or wood. Each polynomial on
interval [ti , ti + 1] is usually of degree 3, being defined by
the location of the extremities Pi and Pi+1 , as are the tangent
vectors at these points, thus ensuring a C1 continuity of 𝛾.
To define a tangent vector at each point Pi , several methods
are available (Farin, 2002). In the Catmull–Rom approach,

aspect to the whole curve.

Unit discrete surface remeshing

A discrete surface is a surface triangulation made up
of triangles and/or quads. As in the previous section,
initially a geometrical support (interpolating surface) must
be constructed. In contrast to the curve case, a smooth
interpolating surface may have artifacts, typically shape
oscillations, due to continuity blending constraints. We
propose violating the smoothness constraint in order to
define an “almost smooth” geometrical support, which is, in
practice, sufficient for remeshing purposes.
To construct the geometrical support, first, the edges representing the ridges and the vertices that are corners (singularities) are identified (example.g., using dihedral facet angles).
To each edge (internal or ridge) we associate an interpolating
cubic Bézier curve segment by using the two endpoints and
the normals at these points (Walton and Meek, 1996). Hence,
a curve network is established over the surface triangulation. Each element is then equipped with its boundary control
points. Such an element can be seen as a serendipity element
(Arnold and Awanou, 2011), and a complete cubic Bézier
patch (Bézier, 1986), can be constructed by inventing the
missing internal control points (George et al., 2014a, b). By
construction, the interpolating surface is not globally smooth
but is almost smooth to serve as an adequate geometrical
support (with no artefacts).
The remeshing of the discrete surface consists, as above in
the case of curve rediscretization, in iteratively optimizing
the lengths of the edges. In addition, topology modifications
by means of edge flipping are also applied. The edge flip is
performed if the geometry is preserved (to improve quality
criteria) or improved (the alternative solution being closer to
the support while the shape quality criteria remain acceptable). All the new vertices are mapped on the support. The

HIGH-ORDER (CURVED) MESHES
(GEOMETRIC VALIDATION AND

High-order meshes (Oden, 1994; Dey et al., 1999; Sherwin
and Peiro, 2002; Xuo et al., 2004; Cottrell et al., 2009; Sahni
et al., 2010; Johnen et al., 2014) offer two attractive features.
On the one hand, the geometry of the (curved) boundaries
is approached with a greater accuracy (and, possibly, with
less elements) than with a piecewise linear approximation.
On the other hand, it is necessary to be consistent with the
order of the numerical methods used in the solution step so
as to recover its theoretical order (Bassi and Rebay, 1997).
Curved meshes are constructed by modifying an initial
linear mesh. This involves creating the nodes in the element
edges, the element faces and, when necessary, inside the
elements. This step may result in invalid and/or low quality
elements so we need a way to detect such elements prior to
correct them.
To give a comprehensive idea of what to do, we consider
Lagrange finite elements in two dimensions. For such
elements, the current element is the mapping of a reference
element whose nodes are the image of the reference nodes.
The way to decide whether an element is valid or invalid is
to make sure that the Jacobian polynomial of the mapping
is strictly positive everywhere in the element. To that end, it
is necessary to find conditions such that the positiveness is
guaranteed. Since we are discussing Lagrange elements, an
easy way to find these conditions is to rewrite the Lagrange
elements in terms of a Bézier form where the Bernstein
polynomials (Bézier, 1986; Farin, 2002), act as a basis of
the polynomial space (therefore, a simple change in basis).

Mesh construction

As previously mentioned, the targeted high-order mesh
is obtained by modifying a linear mesh provided by any
mesh generation method (Johnen et al., 2011; George and
Borouchaki, 2012; Gargallo-Peiro et al., 2015), and so on.
Nevertheless, since the high-order mesh inherits from the
linear mesh, the characteristics of the latter will impact on
the final result. This concerns both the size of the elements
and their shapes (and can be anticipated to some extent).
The second step of the construction method is to add the
nodes where needed (edges, faces, and inside the elements if
necessary) while conforming to the geometry. The boundary
nodes are those of the input surface mesh (when this is the
data we have in hand) or are defined using the CAD system
used for the geometry definition.
The third step concerns the geometric validity. When
adding the boundary nodes, the mesh is deformed and invalid
elements can be created. Invalid elements are detected using
the conditions described in the previous paragraphs and,
then, are corrected. The geometric meaning of the control
coefficients gives infomation about the local configuration of
the element under analysis. As an example, a corner coefficient controls, in two dimensions, the two incident tangents.
The correction step consists in constructing a one-to-one
mapping between the initial mesh and the deformed mesh.
This mapping is then used to relocate the internal nodes to
achieve the geometric validity. To generate such a mapping,
various methods can be used including deformation calculation using mechanical elasticity or; using radial basis functions which result in a better distribution of the internal
nodes.

11.2

Let us consider a bounded domain Ω described by its
boundary surface Γ. In the context of a numerical simulation
performed using a mesh of Ω as a spatial support, we suggest
a general scheme including an adaptive meshing loop of Ω.
This scheme is made up of two distinct parts. The first part
only involves generating an initial mesh of the computational
domain Ω. The second part concerns an adaptation loop
including the computation, the error estimation, the generation of adapted meshes and the solution interpolation from
one mesh to the other. The method advocated in what follows
is an h-method where the parameter of adaptation is h, that is,
the size (or the directional sizes) of the mesh elements. Other
adaptation methods exist including p-methods, hp-methods,
r-methods, and hierarchical methods, as well as some others
(such as local refinement).

A boundary layer (BL) mesh is a quasi-structured layered
mesh around a given geometry. These meshes are widely
used in CFD (Löhner, 1993; Pirzadeh, 1994; Hassan et al.,
1996; Garimella et al., 2007; Vassberg et al., 2003), and
computational structural mechanics (CSM) to track BL
features, that is, when a solution field has a strong variation
along the normal direction to the surface. The first difficulty
is to deal with the very high aspect ratios of the elements,
O(103 − 106 ), along with a high-fidelity geometry as are
required for various cases: landing gear, full aircraft, and
so on. Several preprocessings of the geometry are usually
needed to define proper directions of extrusion and obtain
a smooth transition between layers on complex geometries
(Garimella and Shephard, 2000; Ito and Nakahashi, 2002,
2006; Aubry and Löhner, 2009).

There are two main types of techniques that can be classified as open or closed advancing-layer methods for generating valid nonintersecting BL meshes.
Open-type method With an open-type advancing-layer
method (Pirzadeh, 1994; Marcum, 1995, 1996; Sharov and
Nakahashi, 1998; Löhner, 1999b; Garimella and Shephard,
2000; Aubry and Löhner, 2009; Loseille and Löhner, 2011),
a boundary surface mesh is the starting point and it is inflated
or advanced one or more layers at a time. Proximity and
intersection checks are performed as each layer is generated. If the criteria for these checks are not met then BL
advancement is terminated locally. The key to a successful
open-type method is a robust proximity and intersection
checking process. Typically, the layers are advanced until
a termination criterion is reached, such as a set normal
spacing or a number of layers. After all layers have been
advanced as far as possible, the inflated boundary surface
is used to generate a tetrahedral volume mesh outside
the BL region, using a generic method (Section 3). The
primary advantage of this method is efficiency. BL regions
with tens or even hundreds of millions of elements can
be generated on common computers, including laptops.
The key weakness is potential intersections of merging
layers. To deal with this robustly requires trade-offs in
efficiency and/or early termination of the BL advancement. Intersection checks of varying sophistication must
be used.

11.3

General diagram of an adaptive computation

Given an initial mesh (as seen above), the general adaptive
computation loop can be developed. Adaptive methods have
become a popular tool in Computational Mechanics and
CFDs. It is generally used to improve the prediction of
complex phenomena involving anisotropic features (discontinuities in the media, fractures, shocks, unsteadiness, etc.)
while minimizing the CPU cost. This process is driven
by an error estimate that predicts the error between the
(unknown) continuous solution and the solution provided
by the solver (Babuska and Aziz, 1976; Babuska and Guo,
1988; Babuska and Guo, 1996; Bank, 1997; Apel, 1999). A
new mesh is generated to minimize this error. The technique
of modifying only the size, the orientation, and the gradation
of the mesh while keeping the interpolation error unchanged
is referred to as h-adaptation and the following focuses on
such h-adaptation methods.

Solution interpolation and conservative solution
interpolation At each remeshing, the solution needs to
be transferred from the previous mesh to the next one (to
pursue the computation). This is a twofold process. First, the
vertices of the new mesh are located in the background mesh,
that is, the elements of the background mesh containing the
new mesh vertices are identified. Second, an interpolation
scheme is applied for each vertex to recover the solution.
The easiest scheme is the linear interpolation using the
barycentrics of the vertex with respect to the element.
This stage becomes crucial in the context of unsteady problems and even more so if a large number of transfers is
performed, as the error introduced by this stage can spoil
the overall accuracy of the solution. In the context of the
resolution by a second-order numerical scheme of a PDE
system of conservation laws, such as the compressible Euler
system, it seems mandatory for the interpolation method to
satisfy the following properties in order to obtain a consistent
mesh adaptation scheme: (i) mass conservation, (ii) ℙ1 exactness preserving the second order of the adaptive strategy,
and (iii) verify the maximum principle. In this context, more
advanced schemes have to be devised.
The mass conservation property of the interpolation
operator is achieved by local mesh intersections, that
is, intersections are performed at the element level. The
use of mesh intersection to build a conservative interpolation process seems natural for unrelated – a fortiori
non-embedded – meshes. The locality is primordial for efficiency and robustness. The idea is to find, for each element
of the new mesh, its geometric intersection with all the
elements of the background mesh it overlaps and to mesh
this geometric intersection with simplices. We are then able
to use a Gauss quadrature formula to exactly compute the
mass that has been locally transferred. High-order accuracy
is obtained by reconstructing the gradient of the solution

12 MOVING MESHES: RIGID AND
DEFORMABLE BODY MOTION
The recent development of computing capacities has made
it possible to run increasingly complex simulations where
moving bodies interact with an ambient fluid in an unsteady
way (Hassan et al., 2007). However, engineers are still far
from performing such simulations on a daily basis, largely
due to the difficulty of handling the moving meshes induced
by the moving geometries. Three leading methodologies
have been proposed in the literature to handle geometry
displacements during numerical simulations.
In the first class of methods, the time-evolving computational domain is treated with a body-fitted approach (Batina,
1990), which means that the computational mesh follows
time-evolving geometries in their movement. In other words,
dynamic bodies have to be moved inside the computational
mesh, and we are faced with a moving mesh problem.
The second class of methods is the Chimera method
(Benek et al., 1985). In this case, each moving body has its
own body-fitted grid. The simulation is then performed on
multiple grids and interpolation stages are performed in overlapping areas.
The last class of methods, immersed/embedded boundary
methods (Peskin, 1972), consists of computing flows on
non-body-fitted meshes in which wet surfaces are embedded.
A special treatment of the elements and points close to
the surfaces and/or inside the body is applied. Moving the
geometry is achieved by relocalizing the embedded surfaces
in the fixed computational mesh.

This section describes a moving mesh strategy for
body-fitted problems where the inner vertices of the mesh
are moved following the moving boundaries in order to
preserve the validity of the mesh. The proposed moving
mesh algorithm can be summarized as

12.1

Mesh deformation method

During the mesh deformation step, a displacement field is
computed for the whole computational domain, given the
displacement of its boundaries. Trajectories can thus be
assigned to inner vertices. Several techniques can be found
to compute this displacement field: implicit or direct interpolation (Luke et al., 2012), or solving PDEs – the most
common of which being Laplacian smoothing (Löhner and
Yang, 1996), a spring analogy (Batina, 1990) and a linear
elasticity analogy (Baker and Cavallo, 1999). The choice of
method depends on the problem at hand, its flexibility and its
robustness.
For instance, in the last method, the computational domain
is assimilated to a soft elastic material, which is deformed
by the displacement of its boundaries. An advantage of
elasticity-like methods is the opportunity they offer to adapt
the local material properties of the mesh, especially its
stiffness, according to the distortion and efforts borne by
each element. In particular, the stiffness of the elements is
increased for small elements, in order to limit their distortion.
More details can be found in Alauzet (2014).

12.2

Local mesh optimization

In order to preserve the mesh quality between two mesh
deformation computations, it has been proposed (Alauzet,
2014), to couple mesh deformation with local mesh optimization using smoothing and generalized swapping
(Section 8.3) to efficiently achieve large displacement
in moving mesh applications. In both cases, element quality
criteria are analyzed not only in the current position but
also in future positions. Indeed, knowing the trajectories of
all the vertices, a mesh optimization must not produce an
invalid mesh in the future.
Mesh smoothing helps to preserve nicely shaped elements
but it is really less efficient in 3D than in 2D. Moreover, it cannot solve the shearing issue. To significantly
enhance mesh optimization, connectivity changes are absolutely essential.
The generalized swap operator is used to improve mesh
quality. The following strategy is considered. Tetrahedra are
treated in order of their quality from the worst to the best one.
For each element, four face swaps and six edge swaps are
simulated, then the best of all the configurations is selected
and performed if it satisfies all the specified criteria in terms
of quality. A key to performing efficient swaps in the moving
mesh context is to allow a slight quality degradation in the
future.

LARGE-SIZE PROBLEMS AND
PARALLELISM

Large-size problems are mainly encountered in some
complex CFD calculations even when adaptive methods are
used. Parallel technologies are therefore the only solution
to handle such problems. More broadly, as hardware technology provides processors with an increasing number of
cores and graphics processing unit (GPU) facilities, software must be revisited to take advantage of these advances.
Parallelism may be included in various computational steps.
Mesh construction, together with solution methods, may
benefit to some extent from parallelism. The first aspect
involves constructing the mesh in parallel, while the second
point concerns parallelizing solvers.
At the time of this writing, GPU computing is far from
being a mature technology and suffers from many drawbacks. Indeed, it is efficient only when dealing with static
data. Therefore, it is best suited for numerical computations
and not really for meshing or remeshing purposes when topological modifications are involved.

13.1

Parallel meshing methods

There are essentially two different ways to construct a mesh
in parallel following either an a posteriori or an a priori
approach. In the first approach, a given mesh of the domain
is subdivided into a number of meshed regions such that
the number of elements is well balanced from one processor
(or core) to another while minimizing the number of nodes
at the processor (region) interfaces. Other criteria related
to the problem at hand can be added to the above classical requirements. Various strategies have been proposed
to achieve these goals. After this domain decomposition,
first, interfaces between regions are meshed in parallel, then
regions are considered to be meshed in parallel (Shostko
and Löhner, 1995; Aliabadi and Tezduyar, 1995; Weatherill
et al., 1998; Löhner, 2001). Such an approach involves a
serial meshing method (while parallel meshing methods also
exist). The second approach no longer considers a domain
mesh but, in its place, defines the subdivision using only
the boundary discretizations. The domain is usually represented by multiple volumes, where each volume is bounded

of the interfaces (as part of the volume mesh faces) and the
second approach avoids meshing the full domain (generally
with a coarse mesh).

13.2

Parallel solvers

While various paradigms are used in the solvers, the main
issue is the proper management of the interfaces from region
to region. This requires communication between meshes. In
this respect, minimizing the node interface as well as insuring
some degree of smoothness at the interface level are of great
importance. Also of interest is load balancing to avoid having
idle processors (i.e., waiting for others). Balancing the load
necessitates a priori evaluating the number of elements
in each region, which is quite easy when using the first
approach, whereas it has proved to be a tedious task in the
second approach.

13.3

A touch of parallelism paradigms

There are fundamentally two different kinds of parallelism:
distributed or shared memory. They are very similar from an
algorithmic point of view since the basic idea is to split the
global work into smaller chunks of equal workload that can
be processed concurrently by several processors. They differ,
however, in the way data is stored and handled.
On distributed memory architectures, data has to be split
or duplicated and sent to the different computing nodes. A
process running on a given node can only access the data
stored on this node. If it needs to access memory from
another one, it has to explicitly request the data via some
system call (MPI).
On shared memory machines, every processor has access
to the whole data, which makes the development of parallel
codes easier. However, when it comes to concurrent memory

writes, there may be issues when two processes need to write
at the same time on the same memory location. This is the
so-called memory race problem. The available tools that help
parallelize code mostly differ in the way they handle this
problem:

Compilers with automatic parallelization features like
Gnu compiler collection (GCC) or intel C compiler
(ICC) try to identify which portions of the code perform
only concurrent memory reads, and can safely be
processed concurrently, and which parts may present
memory races and should be processed sequentially.
They favor simplicity and automation over efficiency.
OpenMP is a language extension that detects these
writing issues and, in some situations, can automatically
duplicate those contended memory regions so they
could be processed concurrently. Some post processing
is needed in order to gather the local results. This
scheme, known as scatter/gather, is more efficient than
the previous one but requires more memory and more
development time.
Memory locking allows for high performance without
memory duplication by cleverly locking some parts of
the memory to prevent other processes from accessing
it during a short period of time. This method cannot be
automated and has to be tailormade for each algorithm.
However, there exist libraries that handle concurrent data
structures and hide the complexity from the user. Such
libraries are not general purpose but are specific to an
application domain (sound, graphics, graphs, meshing,
etc.)

Software that deals with mesh data structures, like mesh

to be evaluated to decide what to do. In the present case,
the predicate is a 3-degree polynomial (a volume) and its

predicate to decide whether a point falls within a disc
(bounded by a circle) or a ball (bounded by a sphere).
Clearly a Delaunay-based method uses this kinds of predicate. Apparently, the question turns to the exact evaluation
of these expressions, but this is not so clear (as we will see
below).

Predicates, arithmetic, and robustness

The exact evaluation of a given expression is guaranteed in
some cases. For example, additions and multiplications with
integer entries allow for this, while divisions do not lead to
the same conclusion. But even in the first case, limits exist.
To be convinced of this, we have to return to what a value
is (in the computer). Let b be the number of bits in a variable (currently b ≈ 50). The volume predicate is of degree
b
3. Therefore, limit = 2 3 is the maximum allowable for a
vertex coordinate. In other words and after renormalization,
an exact calculation needs to have the point coordinates less
than this limit and, moreover, to have the smallest distance
between two points equal to 1. These limits give both the
maximal possible number of points according to the directions as well as the minimal distance between two points. We
have introduced, de facto, the separating power or the resolution of the method. This limit is obviously too restrictive
in various cases and, consequently, while elegant a priori, a
method based on the exact evaluation of a predicate is not
adequate in general. Let us consider the in-sphere whose
degree is 5(!) or consider a domain where the overall size is
quite large while the local details are quite small (as in CFD).
We may conclude by saying that exact calculations are
far from being the solution, and that other ideas must be
involved.
In this respect, minimizing the degree of the predicates that
we use is necessarily a good idea and writing the algorithm
with this idea in mind is a solution to enhance the robustness of an algorithm. As an example, the Delaunay criterion
involves the 5-degree in-sphere predicate (say P ∈ Ball(K))

and thus limit = 2 5 , which is so poor! Why not use an imprecise evaluation and then simply use the volume predicate to
secure the algorithm. In this way, we gain two orders! This is
why a given algorithm is sometimes used in conjunction by
other algorithms designed to obtain a more robust process.
To conclude these two asides, while not being definitive,
we have discussed how to make more robust a geometric
calculation and we have pointed out that, in any case, limits
always exist. Note also that arbitrary precision systems exist

Data structures

The choice of data structure to use is of the utmost importance both for efficiency (or even effectiveness) and for
memory occupation. The notion of a data structure is
twofold. Some are basic structures (such as arrays, stacks,
queues, etc.), some are directly related to the precise context
(for instance, how to define an element and make sure
that all the necessary information is available and easily
manageable). On the other hand, it is important to be able to
perform some basic operations very quickly, such as sorting,
hashing, comparisons, and so on
The present discussion about inserting a point in a triangulation gives us some information about which data structures
we might need. To go into some detail, let us consider the
problem in two dimensions. The basic data is the definition
of a point P and a triangle K. Points can be known through
their indices and their two coordinates in accordance,
COORD(1:2,1:np) where np is the number of vertices. As
for the element, a simple triplet of vertex indices appears
to be what we need. Then, formally speaking, a classical
array can be used such as ELEM(1:3,1:ne) where ne is the
number of elements.
As for 1 (P), we mentioned the need for a structure to
make the search faster, and a naive grid(1:64,1:64) may
be used for this. We define a grid (indeed a bucket) whose
boxes encode all the points we have at hand. The grid covers
the domain and is made up of boxes. Given a point, we
find in which box the point falls and we encode this information. This is required to establish a linked list if several
points fall within the same box. Then when a point becomes
an element vertex (after being effectively inserted in  )
we store this information in an array SEED(1:np) (where
SEED(p) = k means that point P of index p is a vertex
of K, the element of index k). Then, given P, we easily
find the guess element K0 not too far from the solution
and, in this way, we minimize the overall cost of the algorithm.
As for 2 (P), we clearly need fast access to the elements
neighboring a given element, that is, the neighboring
relationships. This could also be a simple array such as
NEIG(1:3,1:ne) where NEIG(i, j) = k means that element
of index k is the neighbor of element j sharing facet (edge)
number i of j. Constructing these relationships can be

CONCLUSIONS

In this chapter, we have discussed mesh generation methods
and mesh adaptivity issues for automated planar, surface, and
volume meshing. After a review of classical mesh generation
methods, we have considered adaptive schemes where the
solution must be accurately captured. To this end, meshing
and remeshing techniques have been revisited in order to
be capable of completing high-quality meshes conforming
to these features. Error estimates have therefore been introduced to analyze the solution field at a given stage prior
to being used to complete adapted meshes. Some details
about high-order meshes, large-size meshes, and moving
boundary problems have been given. Application examples
have been shown to demonstrate the various approaches
proposed throughout the chapter.
While mature in a number of engineering fields, current
meshing technologies still need to be investigated to handle
non-simplicial elements [quads, hexes (still a challenge
to date), etc.] as well as nonlinear elements (quadratic
elements and, in particular, higher order elements). Surprisingly, surface meshing has not yet been particularly well
addressed. Robust implementation for anisotropic meshing
(with high ratios of anisotropy) in three dimensions is still
a field of intensive work and constructing BLs (again, with
high ratios of anisotropy in specific for curved geometry)
also promises to be a source of improvement. Meshing problems that include colliding regions are certainly a pertinent
subject of future investigation. Meshes with billion and
billions of elements also raise interesting issues regarding
massively parallel strategies.
