I. INTRODUCTION
Crossflow arises in the boundary layers under the combined effects
of sweep and pressure gradient. The cross-flow velocity component is in
the direction perpendicular to that of the inviscid streamline (Fig. 1),
and a combined profile forms and twists along the wall-normal direction. Crossflow is commonly encountered in the three-dimensional
(3D) boundary layers, such as swept wings,1–3 yawed cones,4–6 and
high-speed flight vehicles with generally 3D configurations.7–9 The
boundary layer is inviscidly unstable owing to the inflectional profile of
cross-flow velocity component. In most of the 3D boundary layers, the
cross-flow instability co-exists with the streamwise instabilities, like the
T–S waves and Mack’s modes. While in contrast with the streamwise
instabilities, the cross-flow instability exhibits unstable disturbances that
are traveling as well as stationary. Linear stability theory (LST) shows
larger amplification rates for the traveling modes than the stationary
ones. Concerning the crossflow-induced boundary-layer transition,
however, stationary waves usually play an important role in lowturbulence environments, while the traveling waves dominate in highturbulence environments.10 Recent evidence suggests that traveling
waves may also dominate under real hypersonic flight conditions.11

Theoretical investigations on model flows have provided deep
insights into the fundamental characteristics of the cross-flow instability, especially in subsonic boundary layers. As the destabilization of
boundary layer starts from the modal growth of low-amplitude disturbances, the primary instability is of great importance to be studied.
The problem of cross-flow instability originated from low-speed swept
wings; thus, most attention has been paid to this problem, see Saric
et al.10 and references therein, whereas the fundamental features of
cross-flow instability are blurred by the effects of wall curvature and
uniqueness of airfoil shape. Attachment-line instability may also affect
the evolvement of cross-flow disturbances, since the cross-flow instability is found to be close to the leading edge. With this in mind,
researchers resort to the swept flat plate, a prototype of swept wing, to
explore the general fundamentals of cross-flow instability. In practice,
a displacement body is often needed to produce pressure gradient on
the plate, which is necessary to give rise to crossflow in boundary
layers.

flow thereafter. The exact solution provides feasibility to study the

The linear instability of compressible boundary layers over a flat plate in the presence of parameterized crossflow has been investigated by
means of linear stability theory. A family of boundary layer with crossflow is obtained as the base flow from the compressible
Falkner–Skan–Cooke (FSC) flow model. Two factors, that is, the local swept angle and the pressure-gradient parameter, are designed to create the crossflow with different directions and magnitudes, which further results in the variation of the cross-flow instability. Modal properties related to the cross-flow instability are emphasized. The association between the cross-flow mode and the Mack’s mode is clarified by
extending the base flow from two dimension to three dimension. The cross-flow instability is discovered to be integrated with the slow-mode
instability, that is, the instability related to the Tollmien–Schlichting (T–S) mode or the first mode, and it can hardly be distinguished as an
individual mode in most cases. The effects of Mach number, pressure gradient, local sweep, and wall temperature are studied. The behaviors
of the cross-flow instability under such effects resemble those of the slow-mode instability in the two-dimensional boundary layers. It is
found that the unstable modes in the three-dimensional boundary layers are mainly affected by the streamwise pressure gradient and the
crossflow per se. Specifically, the first mode is much more sensitive to the crossflow than the second mode. As a result, more marked variations are commonly observed for the first mode in the boundary layers with crossflow.

Concerning the cross-flow instabilities in the subsonic and supersonic boundary layers, most of the attention has been paid to the
boundary layers on generally 3D configurations, which are closer to
practical flight vehicles in geometry. Pruett et al.17 replicated numerically the swept-wing boundary-layer transition experiment of
Cattafesta et al.18 at Mach 3.5. Direct numerical simulation (DNS)
confirmed the dominant stationary cross-flow modes observed in
experiment when surface roughness was introduced, and the comparison of vortex spacing was qualitatively satisfactory. Balakumar and
King19 conducted stability analysis of boundary layers in a swept cylinder and a swept wing at Mach 3. The accumulated amplification rate,
N factor, was used, and it revealed that the traveling disturbances were
generally more unstable than the stationary ones. The result was consistent with that in incompressible boundary layers reported by
Mack.13 Craig and Saric20 investigated cross-flow instability in hypersonic boundary layer experimentally on a yawed cone in a quiet wind
tunnel at Mach 6. The stationary cross-flow instability, in despite lower
amplification than the traveling counterpart according to LST, was
observed dominant in boundary-layer destabilization, which was in
good agreement with that at low speeds. Multiple instabilities were
studied in the hypersonic boundary layers on a yawed cone.5 It was
revealed numerically the enhancing effect of stationary cross-flow
instability on the second Mack mode. Multiple modes were also identified by Moyes et al.21 in the base flow distorted by stationary crossflow.
The behaviors of traveling cross-flow mode and Mack’s second mode
were demonstrated and compared. Paredes et al.22 analyzed the linear
modal instabilities of the hypersonic flow over an elliptic cone. In addition to the symmetric and anti-symmetric attachment-line instability
modes, the stationary and traveling cross-flow modes with low frequencies were discovered coexisting with high-frequency oblique second Mack mode.
Special effort has been made to explore the cross-flow instabilities
in the compressible boundary layers on simplified configurations. Asai
et al.23 compared the cross-flow instability of a subsonic and a supersonic boundary layer. It was revealed that critical crosswise velocity
beyond which cross-flow instability becomes dominant is almost the
same for both boundary layers. Tempelmann et al.24 studied the spatial optimal growth in the 3D boundary layers over a plate, with and
without curvature. They adopted the solution of corrected ordinary
differential equations (ODEs) system as the starting profiles to integrate the full non-similar boundary-layer equations. Local stability
analysis revealed notable stabilizing effect of wall cooling on the crossflow mode in a subsonic boundary layer. Xu et al.25 considered the 3D
boundary layers on a swept flat plate at Mach 6. The boundary-layer
flow was resolved numerically from partial differential equations
(PDEs). It was found that the slow mode (mode S), excited by the slow
acoustic waves, could evolve into traveling cross-flow waves. In comparison, free-stream slow acoustic waves generate unstable Mack
mode in compressible 2D boundary layers, as reported by Ma and
Zhong.26 Recently, Ma and Huang27 studied the nonlinear interactions
of Mack’s second mode and the stationary cross-flow vortex on a blunt
swept plate. A forced stationary disturbance, induced by two secondmode disturbances, was found to differ from a stationary cross-flow
disturbance.
Such understanding about primary instability sheds light on the
research of receptivity,19,25,28 secondary instability,29–32 flow control,33–35 and transition prediction36–38 of the boundary layers

Physical background
A base flow with physical background is needed to reveal
the physical nature of instability. Generally, the base flow
needs to be governed by Navier–Stokes (N–S) equations, or
the ones deduced with proper approximations like
boundary-layer equations. It is inadequate to add a synthetic cross-flow component to a 2D compressible
boundary-layer profile to imitate the typical 3D flow as
depicted in Fig. 1.
Easiness to solve
Base flows under various combinations of flow parameters
need to be calculated to achieve a broad overview of the
cross-flow instability. It is required that the governing equations are easy to solve. On the one hand, this takes much
less time and fewer computing resources to accomplish the
computations of cases with large numbers. On the other
hand, this makes it better to validate, replicate, and communicate. Thereby, a base flow governed by ODEs, rather than
PDEs, would be preferred.
Controllable crossflow
The cross-flow instability relies on the crossflow in the base
flow qualitatively and quantitatively. The emergence and
variation of the instability are closely determined by the
strength of cross-flow component. Therefore, some parameters need to exist in the problem to control the cross-flow
strength, whose range covers zero and moderate extent
encountered in practice.
Simplicity
Boundary-layer instability are usually subject to different
influences like wall curvature, wall suction, pressure gradient, and so on. Although each factor is needed to consider to utmost extent in practical problems, some of
them are extraneous to the fundamentals of stability.
Regarding the cross-flow instability considered in this
work, we are inclined to focus on a simple problem by
excluding the effects of wall curvature, position angles,
and leading-edge bluntness. Wall suction and rotation are
also neglected.

dominated by the cross-flow instability. Numerical simulations17,39,40
and experimental tests20,41,42 have benefited from the theoretical studies as well.
Nevertheless, some common and fundamental issues about
cross-flow instability still need to be figured out. First, both the crossflow mode and the Mack modes exist in compressible 3D boundary
layers, whereas the association and difference have not been elucidated. The boundary-layer stability analyses on a yawed cone5 and an
elliptic cone22 indicated distinct features between the cross-flow instability and Mack’s second-mode instability. However, the comparison
between the evolvements of slow mode in the 2D26 and 3D25 compressible boundary layers implies some connection existing between
the cross-flow mode and the Mack mode. Moreover, the relationship
between traveling the cross-flow mode and Mack’s first mode has not
been addressed, despite that the frequency band of the two modes
overlaps in a large extent. Similar situation exists as well for the traveling cross-flow mode and the T–S mode in low-speed boundary layers.
Second, it is not well understood that how do the stability characteristics change when crossflow plays a role in a 3D boundary layer.
Specifically, the changes of interest include the quantitative variations
of growth rate, unstable region, and dominance of different instabilities with increasing strength of crossflow in the base flow. Perhaps the
most striking phenomenon caused by crossflow is the occurrence of
zero- and negative-frequency cross-flow modes, in which the zerofrequency (stationary) one was proved to be crucial to the secondary
instability and nonlinear interactions.1,17,20,43 The critical value, if it
exists, of crossflow to induce cross-flow mode with non-positive frequency has not been unraveled yet. Third, the significance and characteristic of negative-frequency cross-flow mode have not been
demonstrated in detail. The cross-flow instability with negativefrequency region has been discovered for long.13,29,44 Mack13 pointed
out that the opposite sign of frequency indicated opposite propagation
directions of disturbances, which was consistent with the statement of
Itoh.44 However, the interpretation about negative-frequency mode is
not in direct accordance with the eigensolution of LST equations.
More details need to be disclosed to support a more satisfactory
explanation.
In addition, to shed some light on the general characteristics of
cross-flow instability, analyses are made under different Mach numbers, Reynolds numbers, and temperature boundary conditions.
However, systematic knowledge of cross-flow instability can hardly be
obtained from case-by-case investigations aforementioned. Mack13
presented a comprehensive study of the linear stability of the 2D compressible boundary layers, based on the mean flow with an exact solution. While for the 3D compressible boundary layers, the counterpart
research has been substantially hindered due to the lack of a simplified
solution of laminar base flow. Therefore, an appropriate boundarylayer solution needs to be obtained to initiate the comprehensive stability analysis of the cross-flow instability.

model problem for compressible boundary layers with parametric

crossflow as shown in Fig. 1. The purpose of present work is to illustrate some fundamentals of the crossflow-related instability and to
clarify how it associates with the instabilities already known in the 2D
boundary layers. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.

flow. Corresponding variations of the crossflow-induced instability
characteristics can be expected to appear in the 3D boundary layers.

The boundary layer at Ma ¼ 4.5 is first considered to investigate
the effects of crossflow on Mack’s first and second modes, which are
commonly encountered in the 2D conditions. The flow condition is

Based on the knowledge of eigenmode in 2D compressible
boundary layers, the global method47 is first used to identify the unstable mode from 2D to 3D conditions. Figure 4 shows the spectra of
eigenvalues obtained with the QZ algorithm. A discrete mode with a

B. Effects of Mach number
In the 2D boundary layers, the behaviors of the first and second
modes are well known at varying Mach numbers. The first mode,
which reduces to the T–S wave in subsonic flows,52 exists in the whole
range of Mach number. In comparison, the second mode appears
when Ma > 2.2 and becomes dominant in hypersonic flows. However,
limited understanding has been recognized about the crossflowaffected instabilities in the 3D compressible boundary layers. In this
section, we investigate the Mach-number effect on the instability in
the boundary layers with crossflow.

V. CONCLUDING REMARKS
The linear instability characteristics of the 3D boundary layers
subject to controllable crossflow have been investigated. A base-flow
model on a flat plate is used to create boundary layer with crossflow,
which is parameterized by a pressure-gradient parameter and a local
swept angle. Asymmetry and negativeness of frequency are found to
be fundamental features for the cross-flow instability in the 3D boundary layers. Two theorems, that is, Symmetry Theorem and ConjugateSolution Theorem, are primarily proposed to reveal the intrinsic
details. The symmetric distributions of stability characteristics in the
2D flows are demonstrated by the Symmetry Theorem and the LST
results. The occurrence of the instability asymmetry in a 3D flow is

However, as the T–S/first mode is more sensitive to the destabilizing

Two more findings are also obtained as follows for the
crossflow-affected instabilities. (i) The cross-flow mode is subject

on the T–S mode at low Mach numbers. (ii) Wall heating is
revealed to stabilize the first mode in the 3D hypersonic boundary
layers, which contrasts with the destabilizing effect in the 2D
boundary layers.
The current work focuses on the stability characteristics of the
3D boundary layers with crossflow. The basic features are presented
qualitatively, whereas quantitative results are not emphasized. The
results may serve as foundations to understand and investigate the
nonlinear instability and transition of the 3D boundary layers in vast
applications.
