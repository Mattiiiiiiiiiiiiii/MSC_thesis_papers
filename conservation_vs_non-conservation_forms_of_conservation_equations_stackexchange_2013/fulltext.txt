smooth, then non-conservative may work. For fluids, this is shock-free flows.

If you have shocks, or chemical reactions, or any other sharp interfaces, then you want to use the
conservative form.
There are other considerations. Many real world, engineering situations actually like nonconservative schemes when solving problems with shocks. The classic example is the Murman-Cole
scheme for the transonic potential equations. It contains a switch between a central and upwind
scheme, but it turns out to be non-conservative.
At the time it was introduced, it got incredibly accurate results. Results that were comparable to the
full Navier-Stokes results, despite using the potential equations which contain no viscosity. They
discovered their error and published a new paper, but the results were much "worse" relative to the
original scheme. It turns out the non-conservation introduced an artificial viscosity, making the
equations behave more like the Navier-Stokes equations at a tiny fraction of the cost.
Needless to say, engineers loved this. "Better" results for significantly less cost!

You show the Euler Equations, reduced forms of Navier-Stokes, which are conservation laws for mass,

6

momentum, and energy along with Stoke's hypothesis. Now, conservative/non-conservative has
nothing to do with conservation laws.
In conservative form, you can directly integrate the derivatives once on a control volume and conserve
the flux quantities through the control surfaces (i.e., finite volume method). That's conservative vs.
non-conservative form (the flux).
In CFD algorithms the form has important implications with shock placements and propagation
speeds. For compressible flows, you want to utilize the conservative forms (where you have shocks).

All answers are good, but those do not answer the question. The question is very basic, and reads:

3

Q: when is a mathematical model called conservative and when non-conservative?
A: a mathematical model is called conservative when it directly stems from conservation of
mathematical quantities within a control volume, i.e. from a conservation law.
More in detail, if beside simple conservation (i.e. mass or momentum conservation) we also apply the
chain rule then we obtain a non-conservative mathematical model, so called because it does not
describe anymore conservation of a quantity on a control volume, since it does not directly stem from
a conservation law. But aren't the two formulation equivalent? NO!!! They are equivalent only if the
solution is continuous. We cannot apply the chain rule if the solution is not continuous! Therefore, the
non-conservative form cannot properly describe the propagation of discontinuities (i.e. shocks). See
Toro's book for more details.
Summarizing: the question is about the mathematical model, not about the numerical discretization.
Clearly there is a strong link between the numerical and mathematical model, and all the other
answers are great. But they don't answer the question.

One important reason (maybe not the only one) to the labels "conservative" and "non-conservative"

2

forms of conservation equations is related to their numerical solution. When written in conservative
form, after discretization of the differential equation by a numerical method, such as, the finite
volume method, the resulting algebraic equation still holds the conservation principle, regardless of
the size of the finite volume. In other words, one can still see the balance of the conserved variable
(mass, momentum, species concentration) in the algebraic equation. On the other hand, when an
equation in non-conservative form is discretized by, let´s say, a finite difference or finite element
method, conservation is only guaranteed when the numerical mesh is refined. In this case, the balance
cannot be seen in the algebraic, discretized equation. Therefore, this label only makes sense when
handling conservation equations. It does not make any sense to try to write a differential equation
that has no relation to a conservation principle in conservative or non-conservative form.
